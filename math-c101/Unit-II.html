2.1 Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

In linear algebra, when a square matrix multiplies a vector, the vector usually changes both its magnitude and its direction. But for some special vectors, the direction does not change at all. Only the length changes by a scalar factor. These special vectors are called eigenvectors, and the scaling numbers are called eigenvalues.

Eigenvalue and Eigenvector Definition
Let $A$ be a square matrix of order $n$. A non-zero column vector $x$ is called an eigenvector of matrix $A$ if: $$ A x = \lambda x $$ where $\lambda$ is a scalar number called the eigenvalue of matrix $A$ corresponding to vector $x$.
Characteristic Polynomial and Characteristic Equation
The equation $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ has a non-zero solution vector $x$ if and only if the determinant is zero: $$ p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 $$ This polynomial $p(\lambda)$ is called the characteristic polynomial of matrix $A$, and $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ is called the characteristic equation of matrix $A$.

Solved Examples for Section 2.1

Example 2.1.1: Finding Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a 2x2 Matrix

Question: Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Write characteristic equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$:

$$ \begin{vmatrix} 4-\lambda & 1 \\ 2 & 3-\lambda \end{vmatrix} = 0 \implies (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - (1)(2) = 0 $$ $$ \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 12 - 2 = 0 \implies \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \implies (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2) = 0 $$

The eigenvalues of matrix $A$ are $\lambda_1 = 5$ and $\lambda_2 = 2$.

Step 2: Find eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 5$ by solving $(A - 5I)x = 0$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 4-5 & 1 \\ 2 & 3-5 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

$-x_1 + x_2 = 0 \implies x_1 = x_2$. Taking $x_2 = 1$, eigenvector $x^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step 3: Find eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 2$ by solving $(A - 2I)x = 0$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 4-2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3-2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

$2x_1 + x_2 = 0 \implies x_2 = -2x_1$. Taking $x_1 = 1$, eigenvector $x^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 2.1.2: Eigenvalues of a Triangular Matrix

Question: Find the eigenvalues of matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

Since matrix $A$ is an upper triangular matrix, its determinant $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is the product of its diagonal elements:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (3-\lambda)(5-\lambda)(-2-\lambda) = 0 $$

Thus, eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = 5$, and $\lambda_3 = -2$. For any triangular matrix, eigenvalues are simply its diagonal entries.

2.1 आइगनमान और आइगनवेक्टर (Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors)

जब किसी वर्ग matrix $A$ को एक vector $x$ से गुणा करते हैं, और दिशा नहीं बदलती, केवल लंबाई scalar $\lambda$ से बदलती है: $Ax = \lambda x$, तो vector $x$ को Eigenvector और scalar $\lambda$ को Eigenvalue कहते हैं।

2.3 Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

Theorem 2.1: Cayley-Hamilton Theorem
Statement: Every square matrix $A$ satisfies its own characteristic equation. That is, if characteristic equation is $p(\lambda) = (-1)^n \lambda^n + c_{n-1} \lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c_0 = 0$, then: $$ p(A) = (-1)^n A^n + c_{n-1} A^{n-1} + \dots + c_0 I = 0 $$

Proof:

Step 1: Let $A$ be a square matrix of order $n$. The characteristic matrix is $(A - \lambda I)$. Its elements are polynomials in $\lambda$ of degree at most 1.

Step 2: The adjugate matrix $B(\lambda) = \text{adj}(A - \lambda I)$ has entries which are cofactors of degree at most $n-1$. Therefore, matrix $B(\lambda)$ can be written as a polynomial in $\lambda$ with matrix coefficients:

$$ B(\lambda) = B_{n-1} \lambda^{n-1} + B_{n-2} \lambda^{n-2} + \dots + B_1 \lambda + B_0 $$

Step 3: We know the standard matrix property: $(A - \lambda I) \text{adj}(A - \lambda I) = \det(A - \lambda I) I$.

Substitute $B(\lambda)$ and $p(\lambda) = c_n \lambda^n + c_{n-1} \lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c_0$:

$$ (A - \lambda I)(B_{n-1} \lambda^{n-1} + \dots + B_0) = (c_n \lambda^n + c_{n-1} \lambda^{n-1} + \dots + c_0) I $$

Step 4: Equating coefficients of powers of $\lambda$ on both sides:

$$ -B_{n-1} = c_n I $$ $$ A B_{n-1} - B_{n-2} = c_{n-1} I $$ $$ A B_{n-2} - B_{n-3} = c_{n-2} I $$ $$ \dots $$ $$ A B_0 = c_0 I $$

Step 5: Multiply first equation by $A^n$, second by $A^{n-1}$, and so on, then add all equations:

$$ -A^n B_{n-1} + A^n B_{n-1} - A^{n-1} B_{n-2} + \dots + A B_0 = c_n A^n + c_{n-1} A^{n-1} + \dots + c_0 I $$

Left side cancels out completely to 0. Thus:

$$ c_n A^n + c_{n-1} A^{n-1} + \dots + c_0 I = 0 $$

This proves Cayley-Hamilton Theorem. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 2.3

Example 2.3.1: Verification of Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

Question: Verify Cayley-Hamilton Theorem for matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and compute $A^{-1}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Characteristic equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$:

$$ (1-\lambda)(4-\lambda) - 6 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda - 2 = 0 $$

Step 2: Substitute matrix $A$ in characteristic equation $A^2 - 5A - 2I = 0$:

$$ A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ A^2 - 5A - 2I = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 10 \\ 15 & 22 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 15 & 20 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0 $$

Cayley-Hamilton Theorem is verified!

Step 3: Multiply equation $A^2 - 5A - 2I = 0$ by $A^{-1}$:

$$ A - 5I - 2 A^{-1} = 0 \implies 2 A^{-1} = A - 5I $$ $$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\left( \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} -4 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1.5 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix} $$
