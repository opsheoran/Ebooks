2.1 Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors and Spectrum

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors capture the invariant directional axes of a matrix transformation. When a matrix $A$ operates on an eigenvector $x$, the vector's direction remains unchanged—it is merely scaled by the eigenvalue scalar $\lambda$. This section presents characteristic equations, spectral definitions, and geometric scaling properties.

Figure 2.1: Geometric Scaling of Eigenvector under Matrix Action
An eigenvector x is mapped by matrix A to a parallel vector scaled by factor λ without rotation.
Eigenvalue & Eigenvector Definition
Let $A \in M_{n \times n}(F)$. A non-zero vector $x \in F^n$ ($x \neq 0$) is called an eigenvector of $A$ if there exists a scalar $\lambda \in F$ such that: $$ A x = \lambda x \iff (A - \lambda I) x = 0 $$ The scalar $\lambda$ is called the eigenvalue associated with $x$.
Characteristic Polynomial & Equation
The eigenvalues $\lambda$ are the roots of the characteristic equation: $$ p(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 $$

Five Solved Examples for Section 2.1

Example 2.1.1: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a 2x2 Matrix

Problem: Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Characteristic Equation $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$:

$$ (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 2 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \implies (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 5, \lambda_2 = 2 $$

For $\lambda_1 = 5$: $(A - 5I)x = 0 \implies \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \implies x^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

For $\lambda_2 = 2$: $(A - 2I)x = 0 \implies \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \implies x^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 2.1.2: Eigenvalues of Triangular Matrix

Problem: Find the eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 0 & 5 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

For any upper or lower triangular matrix, the eigenvalues are simply the main diagonal entries!

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (3-\lambda)(5-\lambda)(-2-\lambda) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 3, \lambda_2 = 5, \lambda_3 = -2 $$
Example 2.1.3: Complex Conjugate Eigenvalues of Real Skew-Symmetric Matrix

Problem: Find the eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 4 = 0 \implies \lambda = \pm 2i $$

Real skew-symmetric matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues occurring in complex conjugate pairs.

Example 2.1.4: Eigenvectors of 3x3 Symmetric Matrix

Problem: Find the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda = 3$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ (A - 3I) x = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

From Row 1: $-x_1 + x_2 = 0 \implies x_1 = x_2$. From Row 3: $x_2 - x_3 = 0 \implies x_3 = x_2$.
Setting $x_2 = 1 \implies x = (1, 1, 1)^T$.

Example 2.1.5: Verification of Eigenvalue Spectrum Properties

Problem: Verify that the sum of eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ equals its trace, and product equals its determinant.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 8 = \lambda^2 - 4\lambda - 5 = (\lambda-5)(\lambda+1) = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 5, \lambda_2 = -1 $$ $$ \text{Sum } \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 5 + (-1) = 4 = \text{trace}(A) = 1 + 3 = 4 \text{ (Verified)} $$ $$ \text{Product } \lambda_1 \lambda_2 = (5)(-1) = -5 = \det(A) = (1)(3) - (4)(2) = -5 \text{ (Verified)} $$

2.2 Algebraic Properties of Eigenvalues

Key Spectral Theorems
  • Sum of Eigenvalues: $\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \text{trace}(A) = \sum a_{ii}$.
  • Product of Eigenvalues: $\prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \det(A)$.
  • Eigenvalues of $A^k$: If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, then $\lambda^k$ is an eigenvalue of $A^k$.

Five Solved Examples for Section 2.2

Example 2.2.1: Eigenvalues of Matrix Inverse A^-1

Problem: If $A$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 2, \lambda_2 = 3, \lambda_3 = 5$, find the eigenvalues of $A^{-1}$ and $A^3$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

If $Ax = \lambda x \implies A^{-1} x = \frac{1}{\lambda} x$. Eigenvalues of $A^{-1}$ are $1/2, 1/3, 1/5$.
Eigenvalues of $A^3$ are $2^3 = 8, 3^3 = 27, 5^3 = 125$.

Example 2.2.2: Spectral Radius of Polynomial Matrix P(A)

Problem: Given $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda = 1, -2$, find the eigenvalues of $B = 3A^2 - 2A + 5I$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

For $\lambda_1 = 1$: $\mu_1 = 3(1)^2 - 2(1) + 5 = 6$.
For $\lambda_2 = -2$: $\mu_2 = 3(-2)^2 - 2(-2) + 5 = 12 + 4 + 5 = 21$.
Eigenvalues of $B$ are $6$ and $21$.

Example 2.2.3: Orthogonality of Eigenvectors of Real Symmetric Matrix

Problem: Show that eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues of symmetric $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ are orthogonal.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda = 0 \implies \lambda_1 = 5, \lambda_2 = 0 $$

For $\lambda_1 = 5$: $x^{(1)} = (1, 2)^T$. For $\lambda_2 = 0$: $x^{(2)} = (-2, 1)^T$.
Dot product $x^{(1)} \cdot x^{(2)} = (1)(-2) + (2)(1) = 0 \implies$ Orthogonal!

Example 2.2.4: Eigenvalues of Idempotent Matrix

Problem: Prove that if $A$ is idempotent ($A^2 = A$), its eigenvalues must be 0 or 1.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ A x = \lambda x \implies A^2 x = A(\lambda x) = \lambda A x = \lambda^2 x $$

Since $A^2 = A \implies \lambda^2 x = \lambda x \implies (\lambda^2 - \lambda) x = 0$. Since $x \neq 0 \implies \lambda(\lambda - 1) = 0 \implies \lambda = 0$ or $\lambda = 1$.

Example 2.2.5: Determinant and Trace via Eigenvalues

Problem: A $3 \times 3$ matrix $A$ has trace 6, determinant 6, and one eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = 1$. Find the remaining two eigenvalues.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 6 \implies 1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 6 \implies \lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = 5 $$ $$ \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \lambda_3 = 6 \implies (1) \lambda_2 \lambda_3 = 6 \implies \lambda_2 \lambda_3 = 6 $$

Solving $t^2 - 5t + 6 = 0 \implies (t-2)(t-3) = 0 \implies \lambda_2 = 2, \lambda_3 = 3$.

2.3 Cayley-Hamilton Theorem

Theorem 2.1: Cayley-Hamilton Theorem
Every square matrix $A \in M_{n \times n}(F)$ satisfies its own characteristic equation: $$ p(A) = (-1)^n A^n + c_{n-1} A^{n-1} + \dots + c_1 A + c_0 I = 0 $$

Five Solved Examples for Section 2.3

Example 2.3.1: Cayley-Hamilton Inversion for 2x2 Matrix

Problem: Verify Cayley-Hamilton Theorem for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and compute $A^{-1}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda - 2 = 0 \implies A^2 - 5A - 2I = 0 $$ $$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}(A - 5I) = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 1.5 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix} $$
Example 2.3.2: High Matrix Power A^4 via Cayley-Hamilton

Problem: Find $A^4$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ using Cayley-Hamilton Theorem.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)(2-\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 2 = 0 \implies A^2 = 3A - 2I $$ $$ A^3 = A(3A - 2I) = 3A^2 - 2A = 3(3A - 2I) - 2A = 7A - 6I $$ $$ A^4 = A(7A - 6I) = 7A^2 - 6A = 7(3A - 2I) - 6A = 15A - 14I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 15 \\ 0 & 16 \end{bmatrix} $$
Example 2.3.3: Cayley-Hamilton Verification for 3x3 Matrix

Problem: Verify Cayley-Hamilton for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ p(\lambda) = (\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3) = \lambda^3 - 6\lambda^2 + 11\lambda - 6 = 0 $$ $$ p(A) = A^3 - 6A^2 + 11A - 6I = \text{diag}(1,8,27) - 6\text{diag}(1,4,9) + 11\text{diag}(1,2,3) - 6I = 0 $$
Example 2.3.4: Expressing Polynomial P(A) in Lower Order Terms

Problem: If $A^2 - 4A + 3I = 0$, express $A^5$ as $aA + bI$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Using $\lambda^2 = 4\lambda - 3 \implies \lambda^5 = 121\lambda - 90 \implies A^5 = 121A - 90I$.

Example 2.3.5: Computation of Inverse of 3x3 Matrix via Cayley-Hamilton

Problem: Find $A^{-1}$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = 1 - \lambda^3 = 0 \implies A^3 - I = 0 \implies A^3 = I \implies A^{-1} = A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

2.4 Minimal Polynomial & Multiplicities

For an eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, we distinguish two multiplicities: Algebraic Multiplicity (AM) and Geometric Multiplicity (GM).

Five Solved Examples for Section 2.4

Example 2.4.1: AM vs GM Computation

Problem: Find AM and GM for eigenvalues of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = (\lambda-2)^2 = 0 \implies \lambda = 2 \text{ with } \text{AM}(2) = 2. $$ $$ \text{GM}(2) = 2 - \text{rank}(A - 2I) = 2 - \text{rank}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 2 - 1 = 1. $$

Since $\text{GM}(2) = 1 < \text{AM}(2) = 2$, $A$ is non-diagonalizable.

Example 2.4.2: Minimal Polynomial of Diagonal Matrix

Problem: Find the minimal polynomial $m(\lambda)$ of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ p(\lambda) = (\lambda-2)^2(\lambda-5). $$

Test $m(\lambda) = (\lambda-2)(\lambda-5)$:

$$ (A - 2I)(A - 5I) = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0. $$

Thus $m(\lambda) = (\lambda-2)(\lambda-5)$. Degree is 2.

Example 2.4.3: Minimal Polynomial of Jordan Block

Problem: Find $m(\lambda)$ for $J = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ p(\lambda) = (\lambda-3)^3. \quad m(\lambda) = (\lambda-3)^3 \text{ since } (J-3I)^2 \neq 0. $$
Example 2.4.4: Deriving Minimal Polynomial from Identity Properties

Problem: Show that the minimal polynomial of idempotent $A \neq 0, I$ is $m(\lambda) = \lambda(\lambda-1)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Since $A^2 - A = 0 \implies A(A-I) = 0$. Since $A \neq 0$ and $A \neq I$, no degree 1 polynomial vanishes. Hence $m(\lambda) = \lambda(\lambda-1)$.

Example 2.4.5: GM Calculation for Repeated Eigenvalue

Problem: Find $\text{GM}(\lambda=1)$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ A - 1I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies \text{rank}(A - 1I) = 1 \implies \text{GM}(1) = 3 - 1 = 2. $$

2.5 Diagonalization of Matrices

A matrix $A_{n \times n}$ is diagonalizable if there exists an invertible modal matrix $P$ such that $P^{-1} A P = D$.

Five Solved Examples for Section 2.5

Example 2.5.1: Diagonalization of 2x2 Matrix

Problem: Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and compute $P$ and $D$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 5, \lambda_2 = 2$. Eigenvectors $x^{(1)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, x^{(2)} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$.
Modal matrix $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$, $D = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.
$P^{-1} A P = D$.

Example 2.5.2: Computing Matrix Power A^k via Diagonalization

Problem: Compute $A^{10}$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ A^{10} = P D^{10} P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 2^{10} \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{-3} \begin{bmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Example 2.5.3: Orthogonal Diagonalization of Real Symmetric Matrix

Problem: Orthogonally diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 5, \lambda_2 = 0$. Normalized eigenvectors $q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(1, 2)^T, q_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}(-2, 1)^T$.
Orthogonal modal matrix $Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. $Q^T A Q = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 2.5.4: Testing Non-Diagonalizability

Problem: Show that $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is not diagonalizable.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$\lambda = 1$ with $\text{AM}(1) = 2$. $\text{GM}(1) = 2 - \text{rank}(A-I) = 2 - 1 = 1$. Since $\text{GM}(1) < \text{AM}(1)$, $A$ cannot be diagonalized!

Example 2.5.5: Simultaneous Diagonalization of Commuting Matrices

Problem: Show that commuting matrices $A$ and $B$ share a common set of eigenvectors.

Step-by-Step Solution:

If $A x = \lambda x \implies A(Bx) = B(Ax) = B(\lambda x) = \lambda (Bx)$. Thus $Bx$ is also an eigenvector of $A$ for $\lambda$, leading to simultaneous diagonalizability.

2.6 Quadratic Forms & Classification

A quadratic form is $Q(x) = x^T A x$ where $A$ is a real symmetric matrix.

Five Solved Examples for Section 2.6

Example 2.6.1: Matrix of a Quadratic Form

Problem: Find symmetric matrix $A$ for $Q(x_1, x_2, x_3) = 2x_1^2 + 5x_2^2 + 3x_3^2 + 4x_1 x_2 - 6x_1 x_3 + 8x_2 x_3$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 & -3 \\ 2 & 5 & 4 \\ -3 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$
Example 2.6.2: Classification via Eigenvalues

Problem: Classify $Q(x) = 3x_1^2 + 2x_2^2$ and $Q(y) = y_1^2 - 4y_2^2$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

For $Q(x)$, $\lambda_1 = 3, \lambda_2 = 2 > 0 \implies$ Positive Definite.
For $Q(y)$, $\lambda_1 = 1, \lambda_2 = -4 \implies$ Indefinite.

Example 2.6.3: Canonical Reduction via Orthogonal Transformation

Problem: Reduce $Q = x_1^2 + 4x_1 x_2 + 4x_2^2$ to canonical form.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \implies \lambda_1 = 5, \lambda_2 = 0 \implies Q_{\text{canonical}} = 5 y_1^2 + 0 y_2^2. $$
Example 2.6.4: Sylvester's Criterion for Positive Definiteness

Problem: Test $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ using principal minors.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \Delta_1 = 2 > 0, \quad \Delta_2 = 4 - 1 = 3 > 0 \implies \text{Positive Definite!} $$
Example 2.6.5: Index and Signature of Quadratic Form

Problem: Find index $s$ and signature $2s - r$ for canonical form $Q = 3y_1^2 + 5y_2^2 - 2y_3^2$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Rank $r = 3$. Number of positive terms $s = 2$.
Index $s = 2$. Signature $= 2(2) - 3 = 1$.
