Foundations of Matrix Algebra, Special Matrices, Echelon forms, PAQ Normal Form, Gauss-Jordan Inversion, Rouché-Capelli System Consistency, and LU Decomposition

1.0 Basics of Matrix Algebra

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers arranged in horizontal rows and vertical columns. Matrices provide a compact notation for solving systems of linear equations and representing linear transformations.

1. Definition and Order of a Matrix
An array of $m \times n$ numbers arranged in $m$ horizontal rows and $n$ vertical columns is called a matrix of order $m \times n$. We write matrix $A$ as: $$ A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & \dots & a_{1n} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} & \dots & a_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{m1} & a_{m2} & \dots & a_{mn} \end{bmatrix} = (a_{ij})_{m \times n} $$ where $a_{ij}$ denotes the entry in row $i$ and column $j$.
Basic Types of Matrices (With Illustrated Examples)

Each matrix type is defined below along with a clear numeric example:

1. Row Matrix: A matrix having only one row.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Order } 1 \times 3) $$

2. Column Matrix: A matrix having only one column.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } B = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 0 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Order } 3 \times 1) $$

3. Zero Matrix (Null Matrix): A matrix in which all entries are zero, denoted by $0$.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } 0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Order } 2 \times 3) $$

4. Square Matrix: A matrix in which the number of rows equals the number of columns ($m = n$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{Order } 2 \times 2) $$

5. Diagonal Matrix: A square matrix in which all off-diagonal entries are zero ($a_{ij} = 0$ for all $i \neq j$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } D = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$

6. Scalar Matrix: A diagonal matrix in which all diagonal entries are equal ($a_{ii} = k$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } S = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

7. Identity Matrix (Unit Matrix): A scalar matrix in which all diagonal entries are equal to $1$, denoted by $I_n$.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } I_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

8. Upper Triangular Matrix: A square matrix in which all entries below the main diagonal are zero ($a_{ij} = 0$ for all $i > j$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } U = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 3 & 5 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

9. Lower Triangular Matrix: A square matrix in which all entries above the main diagonal are zero ($a_{ij} = 0$ for all $i < j$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } L = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 4 & 0 \\ 3 & 5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

10. Transpose of a Matrix ($A^T$): The matrix formed by interchanging the rows and columns of matrix $A$.

$$ \text{Example: If Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{then Transpose Matrix } A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix} $$

11. Symmetric Matrix: A square matrix $A$ satisfying $A^T = A$ ($a_{ij} = a_{ji}$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 3 & 5 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = A^T $$

12. Skew-Symmetric Matrix: A square matrix $A$ satisfying $A^T = -A$ ($a_{ij} = -a_{ji}$). Main diagonal elements are zero.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2 & -3 \\ -2 & 0 & 4 \\ 3 & -4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies A^T = -A $$
Theorem 1.0.1: Reversal Law for Transpose of Product Matrices
Statement: If matrix $A$ and matrix $B$ are compatible for multiplication, then: $$ (A B)^T = B^T A^T $$

Proof:

Let matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ be of order $m \times n$, and matrix $B = (b_{jk})$ be of order $n \times p$.

The product matrix $C = A B$ is of order $m \times p$. Its $(i, k)$-th element is:

$$ c_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{jk} $$

The $(k, i)$-th element of transpose matrix $(AB)^T$ is:

$$ ((AB)^T)_{ki} = c_{ik} $$ $$ ((AB)^T)_{ki} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{jk} $$

Now consider matrix $B^T$ of order $p \times n$ and matrix $A^T$ of order $n \times m$.

Let entry of $B^T$ be $b'_{kj} = b_{jk}$, and entry of $A^T$ be $a'_{ji} = a_{ij}$.

The $(k, i)$-th element of product matrix $B^T A^T$ is:

$$ (B^T A^T)_{ki} = \sum_{j=1}^n b'_{kj} a'_{ji} $$ $$ (B^T A^T)_{ki} = \sum_{j=1}^n b_{jk} a_{ij} $$ $$ (B^T A^T)_{ki} = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{jk} $$

Comparing both expressions gives:

$$ ((AB)^T)_{ki} = (B^T A^T)_{ki} $$

Thus, $(A B)^T = B^T A^T$. Proof complete.

Theorem 1.0.2: Expression of Square Matrix as Sum of Symmetric and Skew-Symmetric Matrices
Statement: Every real square matrix $A$ can be uniquely expressed as: $$ A = P + Q $$ $$ \text{where } P = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T) \quad \text{and} \quad Q = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T) $$

Proof:

Step 1: Write matrix $A$ as an identity expansion:

$$ A = \frac{1}{2}(2A) $$ $$ A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T + A - A^T) $$ $$ A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T) + \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T) $$

Set $P = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T)$ and $Q = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T)$.

Step 2: Check symmetry of matrix $P$:

$$ P^T = \left(\frac{1}{2}(A + A^T)\right)^T $$ $$ P^T = \frac{1}{2}(A^T + (A^T)^T) $$ $$ P^T = \frac{1}{2}(A^T + A) $$ $$ P^T = P $$

Thus matrix $P$ is symmetric.

Step 3: Check skew-symmetry of matrix $Q$:

$$ Q^T = \left(\frac{1}{2}(A - A^T)\right)^T $$ $$ Q^T = \frac{1}{2}(A^T - (A^T)^T) $$ $$ Q^T = \frac{1}{2}(A^T - A) $$ $$ Q^T = -Q $$

Thus matrix $Q$ is skew-symmetric.

Step 4: Prove uniqueness. Suppose $A = P_1 + Q_1$ with $P_1^T = P_1$ and $Q_1^T = -Q_1$.

$$ A^T = P_1^T + Q_1^T $$ $$ A^T = P_1 - Q_1 $$

Adding equations $A = P_1 + Q_1$ and $A^T = P_1 - Q_1$ gives:

$$ A + A^T = 2 P_1 $$ $$ P_1 = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T) = P $$

Subtracting $A^T$ from $A$ gives:

$$ A - A^T = 2 Q_1 $$ $$ Q_1 = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T) = Q $$

Thus representation $A = P + Q$ is unique. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 1.0

Example 1.0.1: Verification of Orthogonal Identity for Trigonometric Matrix

Question: If matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha & \sin\alpha \\ -\sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{bmatrix}$, verify that $A A^T = I = A^T A$.

Solution:

Step 1: Write transpose matrix $A^T$:

$$ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Multiply matrix $A$ and matrix $A^T$:

$$ A A^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha & \sin\alpha \\ -\sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ A A^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos^2\alpha + \sin^2\alpha & -\cos\alpha\sin\alpha + \sin\alpha\cos\alpha \\ -\sin\alpha\cos\alpha + \cos\alpha\sin\alpha & \sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ A A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I $$

Step 3: Multiply matrix $A^T$ and matrix $A$:

$$ A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos\alpha & \sin\alpha \\ -\sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I $$

Hence $A A^T = I = A^T A$ is verified.

Example 1.0.2: Decomposing a 3x3 Numeric Matrix into Symmetric and Skew-Symmetric Parts

Question: Express matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 & 6 \\ 2 & 7 & -1 \\ 5 & 4 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ as $A = P + Q$, where matrix $P$ is symmetric and matrix $Q$ is skew-symmetric.

Solution:

Step 1: Write transpose matrix $A^T$:

$$ A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & 5 \\ -2 & 7 & 4 \\ 6 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Compute symmetric matrix $P = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^T)$:

$$ A + A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3+3 & -2+2 & 6+5 \\ 2-2 & 7+7 & -1+4 \\ 5+6 & 4-1 & 0+0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 0 & 11 \\ 0 & 14 & 3 \\ 11 & 3 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ P = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 5.5 \\ 0 & 7 & 1.5 \\ 5.5 & 1.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3: Compute skew-symmetric matrix $Q = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^T)$:

$$ A - A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3-3 & -2-2 & 6-5 \\ 2-(-2) & 7-7 & -1-4 \\ 5-6 & 4-(-1) & 0-0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -4 & 1 \\ 4 & 0 & -5 \\ -1 & 5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 & 0.5 \\ 2 & 0 & -2.5 \\ -0.5 & 2.5 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 4: Verify sum $P + Q$:

$$ P + Q = \begin{bmatrix} 3+0 & 0-2 & 5.5+0.5 \\ 0+2 & 7+0 & 1.5-2.5 \\ 5.5-0.5 & 1.5+2.5 & 0+0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -2 & 6 \\ 2 & 7 & -1 \\ 5 & 4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = A $$

1.1 Types of Special Matrices

Matrices with special algebraic properties play a key role in physics, engineering, and advanced linear algebra.

Definitions of Special Matrices (With Illustrated Examples)

1. Conjugate Transpose ($A^H$ or $A^\dagger$): Formed by taking the complex conjugate of each entry of complex matrix $A$ and then taking its transpose ($A^H = (\overline{A})^T$).

$$ \text{Example: If Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 1+i & 2 \\ -3i & 4-2i \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{then } A^H = \begin{bmatrix} 1-i & 3i \\ 2 & 4+2i \end{bmatrix} $$

2. Hermitian Matrix: A square complex matrix $A$ satisfying $A^H = A$ ($a_{ij} = \overline{a_{ji}}$). Diagonal elements are real.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3+i \\ 3-i & 5 \end{bmatrix} = A^H $$

3. Skew-Hermitian Matrix: A square complex matrix $A$ satisfying $A^H = -A$ ($a_{ij} = -\overline{a_{ji}}$). Diagonal elements are zero or purely imaginary.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 2+i \\ -2+i & 3i \end{bmatrix} \implies A^H = -A $$

4. Unitary Matrix: A square matrix $U$ satisfying $U^H U = I$ ($U^{-1} = U^H$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{bmatrix} \implies U^H U = I $$

5. Orthogonal Matrix: A real square matrix $Q$ satisfying $Q^T Q = I$ ($Q^{-1} = Q^T$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } Q = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \implies Q^T Q = I $$

6. Idempotent Matrix: A square matrix $A$ satisfying $A^2 = A$.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \implies A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = A $$

7. Involutory Matrix: A square matrix $A$ satisfying $A^2 = I$ ($A^{-1} = A$).

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I $$

8. Nilpotent Matrix: A square matrix $N$ satisfying $N^k = 0$ for some positive integer index $k$.

$$ \text{Example: Matrix } N = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \implies N^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \quad (\text{Index } k = 2) $$
Theorem 1.1: Unique Decomposition into Hermitian and Skew-Hermitian Matrices
Statement: Every square complex matrix $A$ can be uniquely written as: $$ A = B + C $$ $$ \text{where } B = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^H) \quad \text{and} \quad C = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^H) $$

Proof:

Step 1: Write matrix $A$ as an identity expansion:

$$ A = \frac{1}{2}(2A) $$ $$ A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^H + A - A^H) $$ $$ A = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^H) + \frac{1}{2}(A - A^H) $$

Set $B = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^H)$ and $C = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^H)$.

Step 2: Verify Hermitian property of matrix $B$:

$$ B^H = \left(\frac{1}{2}(A + A^H)\right)^H $$ $$ B^H = \frac{1}{2}(A^H + (A^H)^H) $$ $$ B^H = \frac{1}{2}(A^H + A) $$ $$ B^H = B $$

Step 3: Verify Skew-Hermitian property of matrix $C$:

$$ C^H = \left(\frac{1}{2}(A - A^H)\right)^H $$ $$ C^H = \frac{1}{2}(A^H - (A^H)^H) $$ $$ C^H = \frac{1}{2}(A^H - A) $$ $$ C^H = -C $$

Step 4: Prove uniqueness. If $A = B_1 + C_1$ with $B_1^H = B_1$ and $C_1^H = -C_1$:

$$ A^H = B_1^H + C_1^H $$ $$ A^H = B_1 - C_1 $$

Adding $A$ and $A^H$ gives:

$$ A + A^H = 2 B_1 $$ $$ B_1 = \frac{1}{2}(A + A^H) = B $$

Subtracting $A^H$ from $A$ gives:

$$ A - A^H = 2 C_1 $$ $$ C_1 = \frac{1}{2}(A - A^H) = C $$

Thus decomposition $A = B + C$ is unique. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 1.1

Example 1.1.1: Verification of Hermitian Matrix

Question: Show that matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2+i \\ 2-i & 5 \end{bmatrix}$ is a Hermitian matrix.

Solution:

Step 1: Take complex conjugate matrix $\overline{A}$:

$$ \overline{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2-i \\ 2+i & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Take transpose of matrix $\overline{A}$ to get $A^H$:

$$ A^H = (\overline{A})^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2+i \\ 2-i & 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3: Compare $A^H$ and $A$:

$$ A^H = A $$

Thus matrix $A$ is a Hermitian matrix. Diagonal elements $3$ and $5$ are real.

Example 1.1.2: Verification of Unitary Matrix

Question: Show that matrix $U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is a Unitary matrix.

Solution:

Step 1: Find conjugate transpose matrix $U^H$:

$$ U^H = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Multiply $U^H$ and $U$:

$$ U^H U = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -i \\ -i & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & i \\ i & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ U^H U = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1-i^2 & i-i \\ -i+i & -i^2+1 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ U^H U = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I $$

Since $U^H U = I$, matrix $U$ is Unitary.

1.2 Matrix Rank and Row Echelon Forms

The rank of a matrix tells us the maximum number of linearly independent rows or columns present in the matrix. We use elementary row operations to reduce a matrix into simple echelon forms.

Elementary Row Operations (EROs)
There are three types of elementary row operations on a matrix:
  1. Interchanging any two rows: $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$.
  2. Multiplying any row by a non-zero constant $k$: $R_i \to k R_i$.
  3. Adding $k$ times row $j$ to row $i$: $R_i \to R_i + k R_j$.
Row Echelon Form (REF) & Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF)
A matrix is in Row Echelon Form (REF) if:
  1. All completely zero rows are placed at the bottom of the matrix.
  2. The first non-zero entry (leading entry or pivot) in a row appears strictly to the right of the pivot in the row above it.
A matrix is in Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF) if additionally:
  1. Every leading entry (pivot) is equal to $1$.
  2. Each column containing a pivot has zeros in all other positions of that column.
Theorem 1.2: Invariance of Matrix Rank under Elementary Row Operations
Statement: Performing elementary row operations on a matrix does not change its rank. The rank $\rho(A)$ of matrix $A$ is equal to the number of non-zero rows in its Row Echelon Form.

Proof:

Let matrix $A$ have row vectors $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_m$. A linear combination of rows is $a_1 r_1 + a_2 r_2 + \dots + a_m r_m = 0$.

1. Interchanging two rows $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$ only changes the order of row vectors. The subspace spanned by rows remains identical.

2. Multiplying row $r_i$ by non-zero constant $k$ gives $k r_i$. Since $k \neq 0$, the row vector $k r_i$ spans the exact same line as $r_i$.

3. Replacing row $r_i$ by $r_i + k r_j$ creates a linear combination. Any vector that was a combination of original rows can still be written using new rows by subtracting $k r_j$.

Therefore, row space dimension remains unchanged under all three operations. Hence, rank $\rho(A)$ is invariant under EROs. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 1.2

Example 1.2.1: Finding Rank of a 3x3 Matrix via Echelon Form

Question: Find the rank of matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 7 \\ 3 & 6 & 10 \end{bmatrix}$ using elementary row operations.

Solution:

Step 1: Perform operations $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1$ to make entries below $a_{11}$ equal to 0:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 - 2(1) & 4 - 2(2) & 7 - 2(3) \\ 3 - 3(1) & 6 - 3(2) & 10 - 3(3) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Perform operation $R_3 \to R_3 - R_2$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 - 0 & 0 - 0 & 1 - 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3: Count non-zero rows in this Row Echelon Form. Row 1 and Row 2 are non-zero. Row 3 is completely zero. Number of non-zero rows is 2. Therefore, rank of matrix $A$ is $\rho(A) = 2$.

Example 1.2.2: RREF Reduction of a 3x4 Matrix

Question: Reduce matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 2 & 0 & 2 \\ 4 & 0 & 4 & 8 \end{bmatrix}$ to Reduced Row Echelon Form (RREF) and find its rank.

Solution:

Step 1: Perform $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 \to R_3 - 4R_1$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 4 & -4 & -4 \\ 0 & 4 & -4 & -4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Perform $R_2 \to \frac{1}{4} R_2$, then $R_3 \to R_3 - 4R_2$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3: Make entry above pivot equal to 0 by $R_1 \to R_1 + R_2$:

$$ \text{RREF} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Number of non-zero rows is 2. Therefore, rank of matrix $A$ is 2.

Example 1.2.3: Parameter-Dependent Matrix Rank

Question: Find the rank of matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & k \\ 1 & 4 & k^2 \end{bmatrix}$ for different real values of parameter $k$.

Solution:

Step 1: Perform $R_2 \to R_2 - R_1$ and $R_3 \to R_3 - R_1$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & k-1 \\ 0 & 3 & k^2-1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Perform $R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_2$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & k-1 \\ 0 & 0 & (k^2-1) - 3(k-1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & k-1 \\ 0 & 0 & (k-1)(k-2) \end{bmatrix} $$

Conclusions:

  • Case 1: If $k = 1$ or $k = 2$, bottom row entry becomes 0. Number of non-zero rows is 2 $\implies \rho(A) = 2$.
  • Case 2: If $k \neq 1$ and $k \neq 2$, all 3 rows are non-zero $\implies \rho(A) = 3$.

1.2 आव्यूह की कोटि और रो एकलोन फॉर्म (Matrix Rank and Row Echelon Forms)

Elementary Row Operations (EROs):

  1. दो पंक्तियों की आपस में अदला-बदली: $R_i \leftrightarrow R_j$।
  2. किसी पंक्ति को अ-शून्य संख्या $k$ से गुणा करना: $R_i \to k R_i$।
  3. किसी पंक्ति में दूसरी पंक्ति का $k$ गुना जोड़ना: $R_i \to R_i + k R_j$।

Rank ($\rho(A)$): Row Echelon Form में अ-शून्य पंक्तियों (non-zero rows) की संख्या को Matrix का Rank कहते हैं।

1.3 Normal Form and PAQ Canonical Reduction

By applying both elementary row operations and elementary column operations, we can reduce any matrix $A$ of rank $r$ into its canonical Normal Form $\begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Theorem 1.3: PAQ Theorem
Statement: For any matrix $A$ of order $m \times n$ and rank $r$, there exist non-singular matrix $P$ of order $m \times m$ and non-singular matrix $Q$ of order $n \times n$ such that: $$ P A Q = \begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Proof:

Step 1: Write matrix $A$ as $A_{m \times n} = I_m A I_n$.

Step 2: Apply elementary row operations on matrix $A$ on the left side and simultaneously on matrix $I_m$ on the right side. This gives row echelon form $A_1 = P_1 A I_n$, where $P_1$ is the product of row operation matrices.

Step 3: Apply elementary column operations on matrix $A_1$ on the left side and simultaneously on matrix $I_n$ on the right side to clear non-pivot entries. This reduces matrix $A_1$ into Normal Form $\begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step 4: Accumulate column operations into matrix $Q$. Since row and column operations preserve non-singularity, matrix $P$ and matrix $Q$ are both non-singular matrices. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 1.3

Example 1.3.1: PAQ Reduction of a 3x3 Matrix

Question: Reduce matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ to Normal Form and find non-singular matrix $P$ and matrix $Q$ such that $PAQ = \begin{bmatrix} I_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Write $A = I_3 A I_3$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & 5 \\ 3 & 4 & 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} A \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Apply EROs $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1, R_3 \to R_3 - 3R_1$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} A \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3: Apply ERO $R_3 \to R_3 - R_2$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} A \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 4: Apply ECOs $C_2 \to C_2 - C_1, C_3 \to C_3 - 2C_1$, then $C_3 \to C_3 - C_2$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} A \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Result: $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $Q = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, Normal Form $\begin{bmatrix} I_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Rank $r = 2$.

1.3 सामान्य रूप और PAQ रिडक्शन (Normal Form and PAQ)

पंक्ति संक्रियाओं (EROs) और स्तंभ संक्रियाओं (ECOs) का उपयोग करके किसी भी matrix $A$ को Normal Form $\begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ में बदला जा सकता है।

1.4 Inverse of Matrix via Elementary Operations

The Gauss-Jordan elimination method computes inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ by setting up augmented matrix $[A \mid I_n]$ and performing row operations until it reduces to $[I_n \mid A^{-1}]$.

Theorem 1.4: Invertibility Condition Theorem
Statement: A square matrix $A$ of order $n$ is invertible if and only if $\text{rank}(A) = n$.

Proof:

Part 1 ($\implies$): Suppose matrix $A$ is invertible.

Then there exists an inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ such that:

$$ A A^{-1} = I_n $$

Taking determinants on both sides:

$$ \det(A A^{-1}) = \det(I_n) $$ $$ \det(A) \cdot \det(A^{-1}) = 1 $$

Since the product is $1$, determinant of matrix $A$ cannot be zero:

$$ \det(A) \neq 0 $$

A matrix with non-zero determinant has full row rank:

$$ \text{rank}(A) = n $$

Part 2 ($\impliedby$): Suppose $\text{rank}(A) = n$.

Since rank is $n$, matrix $A$ can be reduced to identity matrix $I_n$ by elementary row operations:

$$ E_k \dots E_2 E_1 A = I_n $$

Let product matrix $P = E_k \dots E_2 E_1$. Then:

$$ P A = I_n $$

Multiply both sides by inverse $P^{-1}$ on left:

$$ A = P^{-1} I_n $$ $$ A = P^{-1} $$

Taking inverse on both sides:

$$ A^{-1} = P $$ $$ A^{-1} = E_k \dots E_2 E_1 $$

Thus inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ exists and matrix $A$ is invertible. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 1.4

Example 1.4.1: Gauss-Jordan Matrix Inversion

Question: Find inverse of matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ using Gauss-Jordan row operations.

Solution:

Step 1: Form augmented matrix $[A \mid I_3]$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 2: Apply $R_3 \to R_3 - 5R_1$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 & -15 & -5 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 3: Apply $R_3 \to R_3 + 4R_2$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -5 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 4: Clear entries above pivots by $R_2 \to R_2 - 4R_3$ and $R_1 \to R_1 - 3R_3$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 2 & 0 & 16 & -12 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 20 & -15 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -5 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 5: Apply $R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|ccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 20 & -15 & -4 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -5 & 4 & 1 \end{array}\right] $$

Inverse matrix is:

$$ A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

1.5 Systems of Linear Equations

A system of $m$ linear equations in $n$ variables $AX = B$ is analyzed using augmented matrix $[A \mid B]$.

Theorem 1.5: Rouché-Capelli Theorem
Statement:
  1. The linear system $AX = B$ is consistent (has solutions) if and only if: $$ \text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A \mid B]) $$
  2. If $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A \mid B]) = n$ (number of variables), the system has a unique solution.
  3. If $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A \mid B]) = r < n$, the system has infinitely many solutions depending on $n - r$ free parameters.
  4. If $\text{rank}(A) \neq \text{rank}([A \mid B])$, the system is inconsistent (no solution).

Proof:

Let $A = [C_1, C_2, \dots, C_n]$ where $C_j$ are column vectors. The system $AX = B$ can be written as:

$$ x_1 C_1 + x_2 C_2 + \dots + x_n C_n = B $$

This equation states that vector $B$ is a linear combination of columns $C_1, \dots, C_n$.

Vector $B$ belongs to column space of $A$ if and only if adding $B$ to matrix $A$ does not increase the rank of column space. Thus $\text{rank}(A) = \text{rank}([A \mid B])$.

If rank $r = n$, all variables are pivot variables $\implies$ Unique solution. If $r < n$, $n - r$ variables are free variables $\implies$ Infinitely many solutions. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 1.5

Example 1.5.1: Non-Homogeneous System with Unique Solution

Question: Solve the system of linear equations: $x + y + z = 6$, $2x + y - z = 1$, $x + 2y + 2z = 9$.

Solution:

Step 1: Write augmented matrix $[A \mid B]$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 2 & 1 & -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 & 9 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 2: Apply $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$ and $R_3 \to R_3 - R_1$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & -1 & -3 & -11 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 3 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 3: Apply $R_3 \to R_3 + R_2$:

$$ \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 1 & 1 & 6 \\ 0 & -1 & -3 & -11 \\ 0 & 0 & -2 & -8 \end{array}\right] $$

Step 4: Check ranks: $\text{rank}(A) = 3 = \text{rank}([A \mid B]) = n = 3 \implies$ Unique solution.

Step 5: Back substitution:
$-2z = -8 \implies z = 4$.
$-y - 3(4) = -11 \implies -y - 12 = -11 \implies y = 1$.
$x + 1 + 4 = 6 \implies x = 1$.
Solution: $x = 1, y = 1, z = 4$.

Rouché-Capelli प्रमेय के अनुसार $AX = B$ की संगति (consistency) की जांच की जाती है।

1.6 LU Decomposition and Applications

LU decomposition factors a square matrix $A$ into a lower triangular matrix $L$ and an upper triangular matrix $U$ ($A = LU$).

Theorem 1.6: Doolittle LU Factorization Theorem
Statement: If all principal leading minors of square matrix $A$ are non-zero, then matrix $A$ can be uniquely factored into $A = L U$, where $L$ is a unit lower triangular matrix ($l_{ii} = 1$) and $U$ is an upper triangular matrix.

Proof:

During Gaussian elimination without row interchanges, we use row multipliers $m_{ij} = a_{ij}^{(k)} / a_{kk}^{(k)}$ to create zeros below pivot $a_{kk}$.

Matrix operations correspond to lower triangular elimination matrices $E_k \dots E_1 A = U$.

Taking inverse gives $A = (E_1^{-1} \dots E_k^{-1}) U = L U$, where $L = E_1^{-1} \dots E_k^{-1}$ contains lower triangular multipliers $m_{ij}$ with ones on diagonal. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 1.6

Example 1.6.1: Doolittle LU Factorization and System Solution

Question: Factor matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 & 3 \\ 8 & 7 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$ into $A = LU$ using Doolittle method ($l_{ii}=1$) and solve system $A X = B$ for vector $B = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 10 \\ 26 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Set $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $U = \begin{bmatrix} u_{11} & u_{12} & u_{13} \\ 0 & u_{22} & u_{23} \\ 0 & 0 & u_{33} \end{bmatrix}$.

Step 2: Compute elements row by row:
$u_{11}=2, u_{12}=1, u_{13}=1$.
$l_{21} = 4/2 = 2, \quad l_{31} = 8/2 = 4$.
$u_{22} = 3 - 2(1) = 1, \quad u_{23} = 3 - 2(1) = 1$.
$l_{32} = (7 - 4(1))/1 = 3$.
$u_{33} = 9 - (4(1) + 3(1)) = 2$.

$$ L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3: Forward substitution $L Y = B \implies \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} y_1 \\ y_2 \\ y_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 10 \\ 26 \end{bmatrix} \implies y_1 = 4, y_2 = 2, y_3 = 4$.

Step 4: Back substitution $U X = Y \implies \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \implies x_3 = 2, x_2 = 0, x_1 = 1$.

1.7 Solved Numerical Problems

This section provides comprehensive representative numerical problems covering all key concepts of Unit I.

Problem 1: Matrix Inversion using Adjoint Formula

Question: Find the inverse of matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ using the formula $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|} \text{adj}(A)$.

Solution:

Step 1: Compute determinant $\det(A)$:

$$ \det(A) = 2(12 - 2) - 3(16 - 1) + 4(8 - 3) $$ $$ \det(A) = 2(10) - 3(15) + 4(5) $$ $$ \det(A) = 20 - 45 + 20 = -5 $$

Since $\det(A) = -5 \neq 0$, matrix $A$ is non-singular and inverse exists.

Step 2: Compute cofactors of elements of matrix $A$:

$$ C_{11} = +(12 - 2) = 10, \quad C_{12} = -(16 - 1) = -15, \quad C_{13} = +(8 - 3) = 5 $$ $$ C_{21} = -(12 - 8) = -4, \quad C_{22} = +(8 - 4) = 4, \quad C_{23} = -(4 - 3) = -1 $$ $$ C_{31} = +(3 - 12) = -9, \quad C_{32} = -(2 - 16) = 14, \quad C_{33} = +(6 - 12) = -6 $$

Step 3: Form cofactor matrix $C$ and take transpose to find $\text{adj}(A)$:

$$ C = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -15 & 5 \\ -4 & 4 & -1 \\ -9 & 14 & -6 \end{bmatrix} \implies \text{adj}(A) = C^T = \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -4 & -9 \\ -15 & 4 & 14 \\ 5 & -1 & -6 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 4: Compute inverse matrix $A^{-1}$:

$$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-5} \begin{bmatrix} 10 & -4 & -9 \\ -15 & 4 & 14 \\ 5 & -1 & -6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0.8 & 1.8 \\ 3 & -0.8 & -2.8 \\ -1 & 0.2 & 1.2 \end{bmatrix} $$
Problem 2: Solving Matrix Equation AB = C for Matrix B

Question: Given matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 & 1 \\ 5 & 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and product matrix $C = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 6 & 1 \\ 5 & 6 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$, find matrix $B$ such that $A B = C$.

Solution:

Step 1: Multiply matrix equation $A B = C$ by $A^{-1}$ on the left side:

$$ A^{-1} A B = A^{-1} C $$ $$ B = A^{-1} C $$

Step 2: Find determinant $\det(A)$:

$$ \det(A) = 3(12 - 3) - 2(4 - 5) + 2(3 - 15) $$ $$ \det(A) = 3(9) - 2(-1) + 2(-12) = 27 + 2 - 24 = 5 $$

Step 3: Compute adjugate matrix $\text{adj}(A)$:

$$ \text{adj}(A) = \begin{bmatrix} 9 & -2 & -4 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ -12 & 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 9 & -2 & -4 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ -12 & 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 4: Multiply matrix $A^{-1}$ and matrix $C$ to find matrix $B$:

$$ B = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 9 & -2 & -4 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 \\ -12 & 1 & 7 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 6 & 1 \\ 5 & 6 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ B = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 27-2-20 & 36-12-24 & 18-2-16 \\ 3+2-5 & 4+12-6 & 2+2-4 \\ -36+1+35 & -48+6+42 & -24+1+28 \end{bmatrix} $$ $$ B = \frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 10 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
Problem 3: Finding Parameter Values for an Orthogonal Matrix

Question: Find the values of real constants $a, b, c$ such that matrix $Q = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} a & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & b \\ 2 & c & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is an Orthogonal matrix.

Solution:

Step 1: For matrix $Q$ to be orthogonal, $Q^T Q = I$. This means columns $c_1, c_2, c_3$ of matrix $3Q = \begin{bmatrix} a & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & b \\ 2 & c & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ must satisfy $\|c_i\|^2 = 9$ and $c_i \cdot c_j = 0$.

Step 2: Check magnitude of column 1:

$$ a^2 + 2^2 + 2^2 = 9 \implies a^2 + 8 = 9 \implies a^2 = 1 \implies a = \pm 1 $$

Step 3: Check dot product $c_1 \cdot c_2 = 0$:

$$ 2a + 2(1) + 2c = 0 \implies 2a + 2 + 2c = 0 \implies a + c + 1 = 0 $$

If $a = -1$, then $-1 + c + 1 = 0 \implies c = 0$.

If $a = 1$, then $1 + c + 1 = 0 \implies c = -2$. Check magnitude of column 2: $2^2 + 1^2 + c^2 = 4 + 1 + 4 = 9$. So $c = -2$ is consistent!

Step 4: Check dot product $c_1 \cdot c_3 = 0$:

$$ 2a + 2b + 2 = 0 \implies a + b + 1 = 0 \implies b = -2 $$

Values of parameters: $a = 1, b = -2, c = -2$.

Problem 4: Homogeneous Linear System with Non-Trivial Solution

Question: Solve the homogeneous system: $x + 2y + 3z = 0$, $3x + 4y + 4z = 0$, $7x + 10y + 12z = 0$.

Solution:

Step 1: Form matrix $A$ and apply EROs:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 & 4 \\ 7 & 10 & 12 \end{bmatrix} $$

Apply $R_2 \to R_2 - 3R_1$ and $R_3 \to R_3 - 7R_1$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -2 & -5 \\ 0 & -4 & -9 \end{bmatrix} $$

Apply $R_3 \to R_3 - 2R_2$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -2 & -5 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Count non-zero rows: $\text{rank}(A) = 3 = n$ (number of variables).

Since $\text{rank}(A) = n$, system has only the trivial solution: $x = 0, y = 0, z = 0$.
