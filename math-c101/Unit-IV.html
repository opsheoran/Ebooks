Unit IV: Linear Transformations

Linear mappings $T: V \to W$, Kernel $\text{Ker}(T)$, Range $\text{Im}(T)$, Rank-Nullity Theorem, Matrix Representation $[T]_{\beta}^{\gamma}$, Change of Basis Matrix $P$, and Vector Space Isomorphisms $V \cong W$

4.1 Linear Mappings T: V → W

Figure 4.1: Linear Mapping T: V → W Action
Linear mapping T transferring vectors from domain space V to target codomain space W preserving addition and scalar multiplication.
Linear Transformation Definition
A mapping $T: V \to W$ between vector spaces over field $F$ is a linear transformation if: $$ T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v) \quad \text{and} \quad T(c u) = c T(u) \quad (\text{or } T(au + bv) = a T(u) + b T(v)) $$

Five Solved Examples for Section 4.1

Example 4.1.1: Verification of Linear Transformation T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3

Problem: Show that $T(x, y) = (2x + y, x - y, 3y)$ is a linear transformation.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ T(a(x_1,y_1) + b(x_2,y_2)) = T(ax_1+bx_2, ay_1+by_2) $$ $$ = (2(ax_1+bx_2) + (ay_1+by_2), (ax_1+bx_2) - (ay_1+by_2), 3(ay_1+by_2)) $$ $$ = a(2x_1+y_1, x_1-y_1, 3y_1) + b(2x_2+y_2, x_2-y_2, 3y_2) = a T(x_1,y_1) + b T(x_2,y_2). $$

Linearly Verified!

Example 4.1.2: Differentiation Operator D: P_n \to P_{n-1}

Problem: Show that the derivative $D(p(x)) = p'(x)$ is a linear transformation.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ D(a p(x) + b q(x)) = \frac{d}{dx}(a p(x) + b q(x)) = a p'(x) + b q'(x) = a D(p(x)) + b D(q(x)). $$
Example 4.1.3: Non-Linear Mapping Test

Problem: Test if $T(x, y) = (x^2, y)$ is a linear transformation.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$T(2(1, 0)) = T(2, 0) = (4, 0)$, while $2 T(1, 0) = 2(1, 0) = (2, 0) \neq (4, 0)$. Fails scalar multiplication! Not linear.

Example 4.1.4: Reflection Mapping in \mathbb{R}^2

Problem: Show that reflection across the x-axis $T(x, y) = (x, -y)$ is linear.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ T(ax_1+bx_2, ay_1+by_2) = (ax_1+bx_2, -(ay_1+by_2)) = a(x_1, -y_1) + b(x_2, -y_2) = a T(u) + b T(v). $$
Example 4.1.5: Matrix Multiplication Operator T(x) = Ax

Problem: Show that $T(x) = A x$ for matrix $A \in M_{m \times n}$ is linear.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ T(a u + b v) = A(a u + b v) = a A u + b A v = a T(u) + b T(v). $$

4.2 Kernel & Range Spaces

Kernel & Range Definitions
  • Kernel (Null Space): $\text{Ker}(T) = \{v \in V \mid T(v) = 0_W\} \subseteq V$.
  • Range (Image Space): $\text{Im}(T) = \{T(v) \mid v \in V\} \subseteq W$.
Figure 4.2: Kernel Ker(T) and Range Im(T) Subspaces
Ker(T) is mapped to zero vector in W, while Im(T) forms a subspace of target space W.

Five Solved Examples for Section 4.2

Example 4.2.1: Kernel and Range Computation for Projection Map

Problem: For $T(x, y, z) = (x, y, 0)$, find $\text{Ker}(T)$ and $\text{Im}(T)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$\text{Ker}(T) = \{(x,y,z) \mid (x,y,0)=(0,0,0)\} = \{(0,0,z) \mid z \in \mathbb{R}\}$ (z-axis, nullity = 1).
$\text{Im}(T) = \{(x,y,0) \mid x,y \in \mathbb{R}\}$ (xy-plane, rank = 2).

Example 4.2.2: Kernel Basis for T(x,y,z) = (x+y, y+z)

Problem: Find a basis for $\text{Ker}(T)$ where $T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is given by $T(x,y,z) = (x+y, y+z)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ x + y = 0, y + z = 0 \implies x = -y, z = -y \implies (x,y,z) = y(-1, 1, -1). $$

Basis for $\text{Ker}(T) = \{(-1, 1, -1)^T\}$. Nullity $= 1$.

Example 4.2.3: Injectivity Criterion via Kernel

Problem: Prove that $T$ is one-to-one (injective) iff $\text{Ker}(T) = \{0\}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$T(u) = T(v) \iff T(u) - T(v) = 0 \iff T(u-v) = 0 \iff u-v \in \text{Ker}(T)$.
If $\text{Ker}(T) = \{0\} \implies u-v = 0 \implies u = v$. Injective!

Example 4.2.4: Range Basis from Column Space

Problem: Find a basis for $\text{Im}(T)$ for matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Column 2 $= 2 \times$ Column 1. Basis for $\text{Im}(T) = \{(1, 2)^T\}$. Rank $= 1$.

Example 4.2.5: Kernel of Derivative Operator

Problem: Find $\text{Ker}(D)$ for differentiation operator $D: P_n(x) \to P_{n-1}(x)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$D(p(x)) = p'(x) = 0 \iff p(x) = c$ (constant polynomial). $\text{Ker}(D) = \{c \mid c \in \mathbb{R}\}$, Nullity $= 1$.

4.3 Rank-Nullity Theorem

Theorem 4.1: Rank-Nullity Theorem
Let $T: V \to W$ be a linear transformation on finite-dimensional space $V$. Then: $$ \text{rank}(T) + \text{nullity}(T) = \dim V $$ where $\text{rank}(T) = \dim(\text{Im}(T))$ and $\text{nullity}(T) = \dim(\text{Ker}(T))$.

Five Solved Examples for Section 4.3

Example 4.3.1: Verification of Rank-Nullity for T: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2

Problem: Verify Rank-Nullity Theorem for $T(x,y,z) = (x-y, y-z)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

1. $\text{Ker}(T)$: $x=y=z \implies \text{Ker}(T) = \{(1,1,1)^T\} \implies \text{nullity}(T) = 1$.
2. $\text{Im}(T) = \mathbb{R}^2 \implies \text{rank}(T) = 2$.
3. $\text{rank}(T) + \text{nullity}(T) = 2 + 1 = 3 = \dim(\mathbb{R}^3)$. Verified!

Example 4.3.2: Finding Nullity from Matrix Rank

Problem: If $T: \mathbb{R}^5 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ has matrix rank 3, find its nullity.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \text{nullity}(T) = \dim(\mathbb{R}^5) - \text{rank}(T) = 5 - 3 = 2. $$
Example 4.3.3: Non-Existence of Injective Map T: \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^3

Problem: Show that no linear transformation $T: \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ can be one-to-one.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$\text{rank}(T) \le \dim(\mathbb{R}^3) = 3$.
$\text{nullity}(T) = 4 - \text{rank}(T) \ge 4 - 3 = 1 > 0$.
Since $\text{nullity}(T) > 0 \implies \text{Ker}(T) \neq \{0\} \implies T$ cannot be one-to-one!

Example 4.3.4: Non-Existence of Surjective Map T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^4

Problem: Show that no linear map $T: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^4$ can be onto (surjective).

Step-by-Step Solution:

$\text{rank}(T) \le \dim(\mathbb{R}^2) = 2 < \dim(\mathbb{R}^4) = 4$. Cannot be onto!

Example 4.3.5: Rank-Nullity for Polynomial Differential Operator

Problem: Apply Rank-Nullity to $D: P_3(x) \to P_2(x)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$\dim(P_3) = 4$. $\text{nullity}(D) = 1$ (constants). $\text{rank}(D) = 4 - 1 = 3 = \dim(P_2)$. Surjective!

4.4 Matrix Representation [T]_\beta^\gamma

Relative to ordered bases $\beta$ of $V$ and $\gamma$ of $W$, every linear transformation is represented by a unique matrix $[T]_{\beta}^{\gamma}$.

Five Solved Examples for Section 4.4

Example 4.4.1: Matrix Representation of T(x, y) = (2x+y, x-3y)

Problem: Find $[T]_{\beta}$ relative to standard basis $\beta = \{(1,0), (0,1)\}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$T(1,0) = (2, 1)$, $T(0,1) = (1, -3) \implies [T]_{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 4.4.2: Matrix Representation relative to Non-Standard Basis

Problem: Find $[T]_{\beta}$ for $T(x,y) = (x+y, 2x)$ with $\beta = \{(1,1), (1,-1)\}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$T(1,1) = (2, 2) = 2(1,1) + 0(1,-1) \implies$ Column 1 $= (2, 0)^T$.
$T(1,-1) = (0, 2) = 1(1,1) - 1(1,-1) \implies$ Column 2 $= (1, -1)^T$.
$[T]_{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 4.4.3: Matrix Representation of Differentiation Operator

Problem: Find matrix of $D: P_2(x) \to P_1(x)$ relative to bases $\beta = \{1, x, x^2\}$ and $\gamma = \{1, x\}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$D(1) = 0 = 0(1) + 0(x)$, $D(x) = 1 = 1(1) + 0(x)$, $D(x^2) = 2x = 0(1) + 2(x)$.
$[D]_{\beta}^{\gamma} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 4.4.4: Rotation Matrix in \mathbb{R}^2

Problem: Find matrix of rotation by angle $\theta$ counterclockwise.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$T(1,0) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, $T(0,1) = (-\sin\theta, \cos\theta) \implies [T] = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 4.4.5: Reconstructing T from Matrix Representation

Problem: If $[T]_{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ relative to standard basis, find formula for $T(x, y)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ T\begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x\\y\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} x + 2y \\ 3x + 4y \end{bmatrix} \implies T(x,y) = (x+2y, 3x+4y). $$

4.5 Change of Basis & Similarity

Change of basis transformation matrix $P$: $[T]_{\beta'} = P^{-1} [T]_{\beta} P$.

Five Solved Examples for Section 4.5

Example 4.5.1: Transition Matrix P Computation

Problem: Find transition matrix $P$ from standard basis $\beta = \{(1,0),(0,1)\}$ to $\beta' = \{(1,2),(3,4)\}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ P = [I]_{\beta'}^{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}. $$
Example 4.5.2: Similarity Transformation [T]_{\beta'} = P^-1 [T]_{\beta} P

Problem: Given $[T]_{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find $[T]_{\beta'}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \implies [T]_{\beta'} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$
Example 4.5.3: Trace and Determinant Invariance under Similarity

Problem: Show that similar matrices $A$ and $B = P^{-1}AP$ have the same trace and determinant.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(B) = \det(P^{-1})\det(A)\det(P) = \det(A). $$ $$ \text{tr}(B) = \text{tr}(P^{-1} A P) = \text{tr}(A P P^{-1}) = \text{tr}(A). $$
Example 4.5.4: Coordinate Change Vector Transformation

Problem: If $[v]_{\beta} = (5, 2)^T$ and $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find $[v]_{\beta'}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ [v]_{\beta'} = P^{-1} [v]_{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. $$
Example 4.5.5: Eigenvalue Invariance under Basis Change

Problem: Prove that similar matrices share identical characteristic polynomials and eigenvalues.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \det(P^{-1}AP - \lambda I) = \det(P^{-1}(A - \lambda I)P) = \det(A - \lambda I). $$

4.6 Vector Space Isomorphisms

Theorem 4.2: Fundamental Theorem of Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces
Two finite-dimensional vector spaces $V$ and $W$ over field $F$ are isomorphic ($V \cong W$) if and only if: $$ \dim V = \dim W $$

Five Solved Examples for Section 4.6

Example 4.6.1: Isomorphism \mathbb{R}^4 \cong M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})

Problem: Construct an explicit isomorphism between $\mathbb{R}^4$ and $M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ T(a, b, c, d) = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} $$

Bijective linear map $\implies \mathbb{R}^4 \cong M_{2 \times 2}(\mathbb{R})$ since both have dimension 4.

Example 4.6.2: Isomorphism P_n(x) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n+1}

Problem: Show that $P_n(x) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$T(a_0 + a_1 x + \dots + a_n x^n) = (a_0, a_1, \dots, a_n)^T$. Bijective linear map! Dimensions equal $n+1$.

Example 4.6.3: Non-Isomorphic Spaces Test

Problem: Test if $V = \mathbb{R}^3$ and $W = M_{3 \times 3}(\mathbb{R})$ are isomorphic.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$\dim V = 3$, $\dim W = 9$. Since $\dim V \neq \dim W$, they are NOT isomorphic!

Example 4.6.4: Inverse Operator T^-1 Verification

Problem: If $T(x,y) = (x+y, x-y)$, find $T^{-1}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Matrix $[T] = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \implies [T^{-1}] = \frac{1}{-2}\begin{bmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 \end{bmatrix}$.
$T^{-1}(u, v) = (0.5u + 0.5v, 0.5u - 0.5v)$.

Example 4.6.5: Isomorphism of Complex Numbers \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}^2

Problem: Show that $\mathbb{C}$ as a real vector space is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$T(a + ib) = (a, b)^T$. Bijective linear map over $\mathbb{R}$. Both have dimension 2.
