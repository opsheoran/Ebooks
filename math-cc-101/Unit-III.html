3.1 Vector Spaces and Subspaces

A vector space $V(F)$ over a field $F$ is a non-empty set of objects called vectors, equipped with two operations: vector addition and scalar multiplication, satisfying ten fundamental algebraic axioms.

Definition 3.1: Vector Space
A set $V$ over field $F$ is a vector space $V(F)$ if under addition $(+)$ and scalar multiplication $(\cdot)$, it satisfies:
  1. Closure under Addition: $u + v \in V$ for all $u, v \in V$.
  2. Associativity of Addition: $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$.
  3. Existence of Zero Vector: $\exists 0 \in V$ such that $v + 0 = v$.
  4. Existence of Additive Inverse: $\forall v \in V, \exists -v \in V$ such that $v + (-v) = 0$.
  5. Commutativity of Addition: $u + v = v + u$.
  6. Closure under Scalar Multiplication: $\alpha v \in V$ for $\alpha \in F, v \in V$.
  7. Distributivity over Vector Addition: $\alpha(u + v) = \alpha u + \alpha v$.
  8. Distributivity over Scalar Addition: $(\alpha + \beta)v = \alpha v + \beta v$.
  9. Associativity of Scalar Multiplication: $(\alpha \beta)v = \alpha(\beta v)$.
  10. Unit Scalar Identity: $1 \cdot v = v$.
Theorem 3.1: Subspace Criterion Theorem
Statement: A non-empty subset $W$ of a vector space $V(F)$ is a subspace of $V$ if and only if for all scalars $\alpha, \beta \in F$ and vectors $u, v \in W$: $$ \alpha u + \beta v \in W $$

Step-by-Step Proof:

Step 1: Necessary Condition ($\Rightarrow$)

Suppose $W$ is a subspace of $V(F)$.

Since $W$ is a vector space in its own right, scalar multiplication is closed in $W$:

$$ \alpha u \in W $$ $$ \beta v \in W $$

By closure under vector addition in $W$:

$$ \alpha u + \beta v \in W $$

Step 2: Sufficient Condition ($\Leftarrow$)

Suppose $\alpha u + \beta v \in W$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in F$ and $u, v \in W$.

Taking $\alpha = 1, \beta = 1$:

$$ 1 \cdot u + 1 \cdot v = u + v \in W $$

This proves closure under addition.

Taking $\alpha = 0, \beta = 0$:

$$ 0 \cdot u + 0 \cdot v = 0 \in W $$

This proves existence of zero vector in $W$.

Taking $\alpha = -1, \beta = 0$:

$$ (-1)u + 0 \cdot v = -u \in W $$

This proves existence of additive inverse in $W$.

Taking $\beta = 0$:

$$ \alpha u + 0 \cdot v = \alpha u \in W $$

This proves closure under scalar multiplication.

All other 6 axioms hold automatically in $W$ because elements of $W$ belong to $V$. Hence $W$ is a subspace of $V(F)$. Proof complete.

Solved Examples for Section 3.1

Example 3.1.1: Verification of Subspace Criterion for Plane in R^3

Question: Test if $W = \{(x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid 2x_1 - x_2 + 3x_3 = 0\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Solution:

Step 1: Check Zero Vector

Substitute $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (0, 0, 0)$ into defining plane equation:

$$ 2(0) - (0) + 3(0) = 0 $$

The zero vector $(0,0,0) \in W$, so $W$ is non-empty.

Step 2: Take Arbitrary Vectors in W

Let $u = (u_1, u_2, u_3) \in W$ and $v = (v_1, v_2, v_3) \in W$. Then:

$$ 2u_1 - u_2 + 3u_3 = 0 $$ $$ 2v_1 - v_2 + 3v_3 = 0 $$

Step 3: Form Linear Combination Vector

For scalars $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, form vector $w = \alpha u + \beta v$:

$$ w = \alpha (u_1, u_2, u_3) + \beta (v_1, v_2, v_3) $$ $$ w = (\alpha u_1 + \beta v_1, \; \alpha u_2 + \beta v_2, \; \alpha u_3 + \beta v_3) $$

Step 4: Check if Linear Combination Satisfies Equation

Substitute components of $w$ into plane equation:

$$ 2(\alpha u_1 + \beta v_1) - (\alpha u_2 + \beta v_2) + 3(\alpha u_3 + \beta v_3) $$ $$ = (2\alpha u_1 - \alpha u_2 + 3\alpha u_3) + (2\beta v_1 - \beta v_2 + 3\beta v_3) $$ $$ = \alpha(2u_1 - u_2 + 3u_3) + \beta(2v_1 - v_2 + 3v_3) $$ $$ = \alpha(0) + \beta(0) = 0 $$

Conclusion:

$$ w = \alpha u + \beta v \in W $$

Hence $W$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Example 3.1.2: Non-Subspace Example (Non-Zero Constant Term)

Question: Test if $W = \{(x_1, x_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x_1 + x_2 + x_3 = 1\}$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Solution:

Step 1: Test Zero Vector Membership

Substitute $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (0, 0, 0)$ into defining equation:

$$ 0 + 0 + 0 = 0 \neq 1 $$

The zero vector $(0,0,0) \notin W$.

Step 2: Test Closure under Addition

Take vectors $u = (1, 0, 0) \in W$ and $v = (0, 1, 0) \in W$:

$$ 1 + 0 + 0 = 1 \implies u \in W $$ $$ 0 + 1 + 0 = 1 \implies v \in W $$

Sum of vectors:

$$ u + v = (1, 1, 0) $$

Check sum components in equation:

$$ 1 + 1 + 0 = 2 \neq 1 $$ $$ \implies u + v \notin W $$

Conclusion:

Since $W$ lacks the zero vector and is not closed under addition:

$$ \text{Result: } W \text{ is NOT a subspace of } \mathbb{R}^3 $$
Example 3.1.3: Subspace of Real Symmetric Matrices

Question: Show that the set $W$ of all $n \times n$ real symmetric matrices ($A^T = A$) is a subspace of matrix space $M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$.

Solution:

Step 1: Check Zero Matrix

$$ 0^T = 0 \implies 0 \in W $$

Step 2: Transpose of Linear Combination

Let $A, B \in W$, which means:

$$ A^T = A $$ $$ B^T = B $$

For scalars $\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}$, evaluate transpose of $\alpha A + \beta B$:

$$ (\alpha A + \beta B)^T = (\alpha A)^T + (\beta B)^T $$ $$ = \alpha A^T + \beta B^T $$ $$ = \alpha A + \beta B $$

Conclusion:

$$ \alpha A + \beta B \in W $$

Thus $W$ is a subspace of $M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$.

Example 3.1.4: Subspace of Real Skew-Symmetric Matrices

Question: Show that the set $W$ of all $n \times n$ real skew-symmetric matrices ($A^T = -A$) is a subspace of $M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$.

Solution:

Step 1: Check Zero Matrix

$$ 0^T = 0 = -0 \implies 0 \in W $$

Step 2: Transpose of Linear Combination

Let $A, B \in W$, which means $A^T = -A$ and $B^T = -B$:

$$ (\alpha A + \beta B)^T = \alpha A^T + \beta B^T $$ $$ = \alpha(-A) + \beta(-B) $$ $$ = -(\alpha A + \beta B) $$

Conclusion:

$$ \alpha A + \beta B \in W $$

Thus $W$ is a subspace of $M_{n \times n}(\mathbb{R})$.

Example 3.1.5: Non-Subspace Example (Quadrants Subset)

Question: Test if $W = \{(x_1, x_2) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x_1 x_2 \geq 0\}$ (first and third quadrants) is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Solution:

Step 1: Check Zero Vector

$$ (0)(0) = 0 \geq 0 \implies (0,0) \in W $$

Step 2: Test Closure under Addition

Take vector $u = (1, 2) \in W$ (since $1 \times 2 = 2 \geq 0$).

Take vector $v = (-3, -1) \in W$ (since $-3 \times -1 = 3 \geq 0$).

Add vectors $u$ and $v$:

$$ u + v = (1 - 3, \; 2 - 1) $$ $$ u + v = (-2, 1) $$

Check component product of sum vector:

$$ (-2)(1) = -2 < 0 $$ $$ \implies u + v \notin W $$

Conclusion:

Since $W$ is not closed under vector addition:

$$ \text{Result: } W \text{ is NOT a subspace of } \mathbb{R}^2 $$
Example 3.1.6: Subspace Intersection Theorem Verification

Question: Prove that if $W_1$ and $W_2$ are subspaces of $V(F)$, then their intersection $W_1 \cap W_2$ is also a subspace of $V(F)$.

Solution:

Step 1: Non-empty Test

$$ 0 \in W_1 \text{ and } 0 \in W_2 $$ $$ \implies 0 \in W_1 \cap W_2 $$

Step 2: Subspace Criterion Test

Let $u, v \in W_1 \cap W_2$ and $\alpha, \beta \in F$:

$$ u, v \in W_1 \implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W_1 $$ $$ u, v \in W_2 \implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W_2 $$ $$ \implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W_1 \cap W_2 $$

Conclusion:

$W_1 \cap W_2$ is a subspace of $V(F)$.

3.2 Linear Combinations and Linear Span

3.2.1 Linear Combinations of Vectors & Linear Span $L(S)$

A vector $v$ is a linear combination of vectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ if $v = c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_k v_k$. The linear span $L(S)$ of a set $S$ is the set of all possible linear combinations of vectors in $S$, forming a subspace of $V$.

3.2.2 Linear Independence & Dependence (Wronskian Test for Function Spaces)

A set of vectors $S = \{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ is linearly independent if $c_1 v_1 + \dots + c_k v_k = 0$ implies $c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$. If non-zero scalars exist such that the sum is zero, the set is linearly dependent.

3.2.3 Basis of a Vector Space & Standard Basis Sets ($\mathbb{R}^n, M_{m \times n}, P_n(x)$)

A subset $B \subset V$ is a basis of $V$ if $B$ is linearly independent and spans $V$ ($L(B) = V$). Every vector in $V$ can be expressed uniquely as a linear combination of basis vectors.

A vector $v \in V$ is a linear combination of vectors $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k \in V$ if there exist scalars $c_1, c_2, \dots, c_k \in F$ such that:

$$ v = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_k v_k $$
Definition 3.2: Linear Span
The set of all linear combinations of a subset $S \subseteq V$ is called the linear span of $S$, denoted by $L(S)$ or $\text{span}(S)$. $$ \text{span}(S) = \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^k c_i v_i \;\middle|\; c_i \in F, v_i \in S \right\} $$

Solved Examples for Section 3.2

Example 3.2.1: Expressing a Vector as a Linear Combination

Question: Express vector $v = (1, 7, -4)$ as a linear combination of vectors $v_1 = (1, 1, 1)$, $v_2 = (0, 1, 1)$, and $v_3 = (0, 0, 1)$.

Solution:

Step 1: Set up Vector Equation

$$ v = c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + c_3 v_3 $$ $$ (1, 7, -4) = c_1(1, 1, 1) + c_2(0, 1, 1) + c_3(0, 0, 1) $$ $$ (1, 7, -4) = (c_1, \; c_1 + c_2, \; c_1 + c_2 + c_3) $$

Step 2: Equate Components

$$ c_1 = 1 $$ $$ c_1 + c_2 = 7 $$ $$ c_1 + c_2 + c_3 = -4 $$

Step 3: Solve System by Back Substitution

From Component 1 equation:

$$ c_1 = 1 $$

From Component 2 equation:

$$ 1 + c_2 = 7 $$ $$ \implies c_2 = 6 $$

From Component 3 equation:

$$ 1 + 6 + c_3 = -4 $$ $$ 7 + c_3 = -4 $$ $$ \implies c_3 = -11 $$

Conclusion:

The vector $v$ expressed as a linear combination is:

$$ (1, 7, -4) = 1 v_1 + 6 v_2 - 11 v_3 $$
Example 3.2.2: Hand Calculation via Gaussian Elimination

Question: Express vector $v = (2, -5, 3)$ as a linear combination of $v_1 = (1, -3, 2)$, $v_2 = (2, -4, -1)$, and $v_3 = (1, -5, 7)$.

Solution:

Step 1: Linear Combination Equation

$$ c_1(1, -3, 2) + c_2(2, -4, -1) + c_3(1, -5, 7) = (2, -5, 3) $$

Form augmented matrix $[A \mid B]$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & \mid & 2 \\ -3 & -4 & -5 & \mid & -5 \\ 2 & -1 & 7 & \mid & 3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 2: Row Operations to Row Echelon Form

Perform $R_2 \to R_2 + 3R_1$ and $R_3 \to R_3 - 2R_1$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & \mid & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 & \mid & 1 \\ 0 & -5 & 5 & \mid & -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Perform $R_3 \to R_3 + \frac{5}{2} R_2$:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & \mid & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & -2 & \mid & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \mid & 1.5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Step 3: Analyze System Consistency

Row 3 equation gives $0 c_1 + 0 c_2 + 0 c_3 = 1.5$, which is impossible ($0 = 1.5$).

Conclusion:

System is inconsistent. Vector $v = (2, -5, 3)$ cannot be expressed as a linear combination of $\{v_1, v_2, v_3\}$.

Example 3.2.3: Testing Membership in Linear Span

Question: Is vector $v = (2, 5, 3)$ in $\text{span}\{v_1, v_2\}$ where $v_1 = (1, 2, 1)$ and $v_2 = (1, 1, 0)$?

Solution:

Step 1: Set up Linear Combination Equation

$$ c_1(1, 2, 1) + c_2(1, 1, 0) = (2, 5, 3) $$ $$ (c_1 + c_2, \; 2c_1 + c_2, \; c_1) = (2, 5, 3) $$

Step 2: Component Equations

$$ c_1 + c_2 = 2 $$ $$ 2c_1 + c_2 = 5 $$ $$ c_1 = 3 $$

Step 3: Solve and Verify Consistency

From Component 3 equation:

$$ c_1 = 3 $$

3.3 Linear Independence and Dependence

3.3.1 Dimension of Vector Spaces & Subspaces

The dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$, denoted $\dim(V)$, is the number of vectors in any basis of $V$. All bases of a finite-dimensional vector space have the exact same number of vectors.

3.3.2 Dimension Sum Theorem $\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim(W_1) + \dim(W_2) - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2)$

The Dimension Sum Theorem states that for any two finite-dimensional subspaces $W_1$ and $W_2$ of $V$: $\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim(W_1) + \dim(W_2) - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2)$.

3.3.3 Direct Sum $W_1 \oplus W_2$ & Unique Vector Decomposition

A vector space $V$ is the direct sum of subspaces $W_1$ and $W_2$, written $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$, if $V = W_1 + W_2$ and $W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$. Every vector $v \in V$ can be decomposed uniquely as $v = w_1 + w_2$.

3.3.4 Quotient Spaces $V/W$ & Quotient Dimension Formula $\dim(V/W) = \dim(V) - \dim(W)$

For a subspace $W \subseteq V$, the quotient space $V/W$ consists of cosets $v + W = \{v + w \mid w \in W\}$. The dimension of the quotient space is given by $\dim(V/W) = \dim(V) - \dim(W)$.

A set of vectors $S = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k\}$ is linearly independent if the equation:

$$ c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_k v_k = 0 $$

implies $c_1 = c_2 = \dots = c_k = 0$. If non-zero scalars exist satisfying the equation, $S$ is linearly dependent.

3.4 Basis and Dimension

3.4.1 Axiomatic Definition of Inner Product Space $\langle u, v angle$

An inner product space is a vector space $V(F)$ equipped with an inner product function $\langle u, v \rangle \in F$ satisfying conjugate symmetry, linearity in the first argument, and positive-definiteness ($\langle v, v \rangle \ge 0$, with equality iff $v = 0$).

3.4.2 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality & Triangle Inequality Proofs

The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality states that for all vectors $u, v$ in an inner product space: $|\langle u, v \rangle| \le \|u\| \|v\|$. This yields the Triangle Inequality: $\|u + v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\|$.

3.4.3 Orthogonality, Orthogonal Complements $W^\perp$ & Generalized Pythagorean Theorem

Two vectors $u, v$ are orthogonal if $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$. The orthogonal complement $W^\perp = \{v \in V \mid \langle v, w \rangle = 0, \forall w \in W\}$ is a subspace satisfying $V = W \oplus W^\perp$.

3.4.4 Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Algorithm & Orthonormal Basis Construction

The Gram-Schmidt process transforms any linearly independent set of vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ into an orthogonal basis $\{u_1, \dots, u_k\}$ and orthonormal basis $\{e_1, \dots, e_k\}$ where $\|e_i\| = 1$.

Definition 3.3: Basis
A subset $B = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ of vector space $V(F)$ is a basis of $V$ if:
  1. $B$ is linearly independent.
  2. $B$ spans $V$ ($\text{span}(B) = V$).
The number of vectors $n$ in basis $B$ is called the dimension of $V$, denoted by $\dim(V) = n$.

3.4 Basis and Dimension (Hinglish व्याख्या)

3.4.1 Axiomatic Definition of Inner Product Space $\langle u, v angle$

An inner product space is a vector space $V(F)$ equipped with an inner product function $\langle u, v \rangle \in F$ satisfying conjugate symmetry, linearity in the first argument, and positive-definiteness ($\langle v, v \rangle \ge 0$, with equality iff $v = 0$).

3.4.2 Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality & Triangle Inequality Proofs

The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality states that for all vectors $u, v$ in an inner product space: $|\langle u, v \rangle| \le \|u\| \|v\|$. This yields the Triangle Inequality: $\|u + v\| \le \|u\| + \|v\|$.

3.4.3 Orthogonality, Orthogonal Complements $W^\perp$ & Generalized Pythagorean Theorem

Two vectors $u, v$ are orthogonal if $\langle u, v \rangle = 0$. The orthogonal complement $W^\perp = \{v \in V \mid \langle v, w \rangle = 0, \forall w \in W\}$ is a subspace satisfying $V = W \oplus W^\perp$.

3.4.4 Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Algorithm & Orthonormal Basis Construction

The Gram-Schmidt process transforms any linearly independent set of vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_k\}$ into an orthogonal basis $\{u_1, \dots, u_k\}$ and orthonormal basis $\{e_1, \dots, e_k\}$ जहाँ $\|e_i\| = 1$.

परिभाषा (Definition) 3.3: Basis
A subset $B = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$ of vector space $V(F)$ is a basis of $V$ if:
  1. $B$ is linearly independent.
  2. $B$ spans $V$ ($\text{span}(B) = V$).
The number of vectors $n$ in basis $B$ is called the dimension of $V$, denoted by $\dim(V) = n$.

3.5 Sum and Direct Sum of Subspaces

Theorem 3.2: Subspace Dimension Sum Theorem
Statement: If $W_1$ and $W_2$ are finite-dimensional subspaces of $V(F)$, then: $$ \dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim(W_1) + \dim(W_2) - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2) $$

$V$ is the direct sum of $W_1$ and $W_2$ (denoted $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$) if $V = W_1 + W_2$ and $W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$. In this case: $$ \dim(W_1 \oplus W_2) = \dim(W_1) + \dim(W_2) $$

3.6 Inner Product Spaces & Gram-Schmidt Process

Definition 3.5: Inner Product Space
An inner product space $V(F)$ is equipped with function $\langle u, v \rangle \in F$ satisfying:
  1. Conjugate Symmetry: $\langle u, v \rangle = \overline{\langle v, u \rangle}$.
  2. Linearity in First Argument: $\langle \alpha u + \beta v, w \rangle = \alpha \langle u, w \rangle + \beta \langle v, w \rangle$.
  3. Positive-Definiteness: $\langle v, v \rangle \geq 0$, and $\langle v, v \rangle = 0 \iff v = 0$.
Theorem 3.3: Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality
Statement: For any vectors $u, v$ in an inner product space: $$ |\langle u, v \rangle| \leq \|u\| \|v\| $$

Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization Process

Given a basis $\{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n\}$, construct orthogonal basis $\{u_1, u_2, \dots, u_n\}$ by:

$$ u_1 = v_1 $$ $$ u_2 = v_2 - \frac{\langle v_2, u_1 \rangle}{\|u_1\|^2} u_1 $$ $$ u_3 = v_3 - \frac{\langle v_3, u_1 \rangle}{\|u_1\|^2} u_1 - \frac{\langle v_3, u_2 \rangle}{\|u_2\|^2} u_2 $$

Solved Examples for Section 3.6

Example 3.6.1: Gram-Schmidt Process in R^3

Question: Apply Gram-Schmidt process to basis $v_1 = (1, 1, 1), v_2 = (0, 1, 1), v_3 = (0, 0, 1)$ with standard inner product.

Solution:

Step 1: First Orthogonal Vector $u_1$

$$ u_1 = v_1 = (1, 1, 1) $$ $$ \|u_1\|^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 = 3 $$

$$ \langle v_2, u_1 \rangle = 0(1) + 1(1) + 1(1) = 2 $$ $$ u_2 = (0, 1, 1) - \frac{2}{3}(1, 1, 1) = \left(-\frac{2}{3}, \; \frac{1}{3}, \; \frac{1}{3}\right) $$ $$ \|u_2\|^2 = \frac{4}{9} + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{9} = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{2}{3} $$

$$ \langle v_3, u_1 \rangle = 1, \quad \langle v_3, u_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{3} $$ $$ u_3 = (0, 0, 1) - \frac{1}{3}(1, 1, 1) - \frac{1/3}{2/3}\left(-\frac{2}{3}, \frac{1}{3}, \frac{1}{3}\right) = \left(0, \; -\frac{1}{2}, \; \frac{1}{2}\right) $$

$$ \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 & k \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 0 \implies 1(2-1) - 2(2-0) + k(1-0) = 0 \implies 1 - 4 + k = 0 \implies k = 3 $$
समस्या (Problem) 4: Dimension of Intersection of Two Subspaces

प्रश्न (Question): $W_1 = \text{span}\{(1,1,0), (0,1,1)\}$, $W_2 = \text{span}\{(1,0,1), (0,1,0)\}$. ज्ञात कीजिए $\dim(W_1 \cap W_2)$.

हल (Solution):

$$ \dim(W_1) = 2, \quad \dim(W_2) = 2, \quad \dim(W_1 + W_2) = 3 $$ $$ \dim(W_1 \cap W_2) = 2 + 2 - 3 = 1 $$
समस्या (Problem) 5: Polynomial Inner Product Space

प्रश्न (Question): ज्ञात कीजिए norm of $f(x) = x$ in inner product space $C[0,1]$ with $\langle f,g \rangle = \int_0^1 fg dx$.

हल (Solution):

$$ \|x\|^2 = \int_0^1 x^2 dx = \frac{1}{3} \implies \|x\| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} $$

Part E: Subjective Questions (10 Questions)

Q1. State and prove the Subspace Criterion Theorem for a subset $W \subseteq V(F)$.
Answer Outline: Show $W$ is subspace iff $\alpha u + \beta v \in W$. Necessity: Subspace is closed under addition & scalar multiplication. Sufficiency: Take $\alpha=1, \beta=1 \implies u+v \in W$; $\alpha=0, \beta=0 \implies 0 \in W$; $\alpha=-1, \beta=0 \implies -u \in W$.
Q2. State and prove the Subspace Dimension Sum Theorem $\dim(W_1 + W_2) = \dim(W_1) + \dim(W_2) - \dim(W_1 \cap W_2)$.
Answer Outline: Let $\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}$ be a basis of $W_1 \cap W_2$. Extend to basis $\{v_1,\dots,v_k, u_1,\dots,u_r\}$ of $W_1$ and $\{v_1,\dots,v_k, w_1,\dots,w_s\}$ of $W_2$. Show $\{v_1,\dots,v_k, u_1,\dots,u_r, w_1,\dots,w_s\}$ is a basis of $W_1 + W_2$. Count vectors: $(k + r + s) = (k + r) + (k + s) - k$.
Q3. State and prove the Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality for inner product spaces.
Answer Outline: If $v=0$, equality holds $0=0$. If $v \neq 0$, let $t = \langle u, v \rangle / \|v\|^2$. Compute $\|u - t v\|^2 = \langle u - t v, u - t v \rangle \geq 0$. Expand: $\|u\|^2 - \frac{|\langle u, v \rangle|^2}{\|v\|^2} \geq 0 \implies |\langle u, v \rangle| \leq \|u\| \|v\|$.
Q4. Explain the step-by-step Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization algorithm.
Answer Outline: Given basis $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$, set $u_1 = v_1$. Compute $u_k = v_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \frac{\langle v_k, u_j \rangle}{\|u_j\|^2} u_j$ for $k=2,\dots,n$. Normalize vectors $w_k = u_k / \|u_k\|$ to obtain orthonormal basis.
Q5. Prove that the intersection of any collection of subspaces of $V(F)$ is a subspace of $V(F)$.
Answer Outline: Let $W = \bigcap W_i$. $0 \in W_i$ for all $i \implies 0 \in W$. For $u, v \in W$ and $\alpha, \beta \in F$, $u, v \in W_i \implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W_i$ for all $i \implies \alpha u + \beta v \in W$. Thus $W$ is a subspace.
Q6. Define Basis Extension Theorem and illustrate with an example in $\mathbb{R}^3$.
Answer Outline: Every linearly independent set in a finite-dimensional vector space can be extended to form a basis. Example: Extend $\{(1,2,0)\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ by adding $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$ to form a basis of 3 independent vectors.
Q7. Prove that any set of orthogonal non-zero vectors in an inner product space is linearly independent.
Answer Outline: Let $\sum c_i v_i = 0$ with $\langle v_i, v_j \rangle = 0$ for $i \neq j$. Take inner product with $v_k$: $\langle \sum c_i v_i, v_k \rangle = c_k \langle v_k, v_k \rangle = 0$. Since $v_k \neq 0$, $\|v_k\|^2 \neq 0 \implies c_k = 0$ for all $k$. Thus independent.
Q8. Define Direct Sum $V = W_1 \oplus W_2$ and prove equivalent condition $W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$.
Answer Outline: Direct sum means every $v \in V$ has unique representation $v = w_1 + w_2$. Uniqueness implies if $w \in W_1 \cap W_2$, $w = w + 0 = 0 + w \implies w = 0$. Thus $W_1 \cap W_2 = \{0\}$.
Q9. State and prove the Triangle Inequality $\|u + v\| \leq \|u\| + \|v\|$ in inner product spaces.
Answer Outline: $\|u + v\|^2 = \langle u+v, u+v \rangle = \|u\|^2 + 2 \text{Re}\langle u, v \rangle + \|v\|^2 \leq \|u\|^2 + 2|\langle u, v \rangle| + \|v\|^2$. Apply Cauchy-Schwarz: $\leq \|u\|^2 + 2\|u\|\|v\| + \|v\|^2 = (\|u\| + \|v\|)^2$. Take square roots.
Q10. Prove that the set of all polynomials of degree at most $n$ forms a vector space of dimension $n+1$.
Answer Outline: Check 10 vector space axioms for addition and scalar multiplication of polynomials. Show $\{1, x, x^2, \dots, x^n\}$ is a basis (linearly independent and spans all $p(x) = \sum a_i x^i$). Count basis elements: $n+1$.
