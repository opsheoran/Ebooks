Unit I: Epidemiology Fundamentals

Principles of epidemiological investigation, health measures, study designs & screening quantitative methods

1.1 Introduction to Epidemiology

Imagine that a single student in your college hostel develops a stomach upset. The doctor examines the student, gives oral rehydration and an antispasmodic, and the student recovers by evening. That is clinical medicine: one patient, one diagnosis, one treatment. But suppose that by the next afternoon, forty students living in Block-A of the same hostel develop vomiting and diarrhoea, while students in Block-B remain perfectly well. Treating each of the forty students is necessary, yet it is not sufficient. Someone must ask why Block-A alone was affected, what was served at breakfast, whether the milk was left uncovered, and how the contaminated food reached only one section of the dining hall. This shift from caring for an individual to understanding the health experience of an entire group is the central purpose of epidemiology.
Formal Definition
Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified human populations, and the application of this study to the prevention and control of health problems.

Four Key Components

  1. Study: Systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data.
  2. Distribution: Pattern of disease by person, place, and time.
  3. Determinants: Causes, risk factors, or exposures that influence health outcomes.
  4. Application: Translating findings into community prevention and control.

Epidemiology vs Clinical Medicine

Aspect Clinical Medicine Epidemiology
Unit of concern Individual patient Population or community
Main question What is wrong with this patient? Why are people in this group getting sick?
Typical action Diagnose and treat Investigate, measure, and prevent
Example Treating one child with dengue Mapping a dengue cluster to remove breeding sites

1.2 Principles of Epidemiologic Investigation: Sequential 8-Step Outbreak Protocol

Step 1

Verify the Outbreak and Diagnosis

Confirm observed cases exceed baseline expected count and validate laboratory diagnostic results.

Step 2

Define a Case and Count Cases

Establish clinical, time, and place criteria to categorize cases as Confirmed, Probable, or Suspected.

Step 3

Search for Additional Cases

Perform active door-to-door case finding to capture mild or unreported cases.

Step 4

Perform Descriptive Analysis

Plot epidemic curves (time), spot maps (place), and demographic rate tables (person).

Step 5

Formulate a Working Hypothesis

Propose plausible transmission vehicle based on descriptive patterns.

Step 6

Test and Refine Hypothesis

Execute analytical case-control ($OR$) or cohort ($RR$) studies.

Step 7

Implement Control Measures

Immediate actions: water chlorination, isolation, vector control, vaccination.

Step 8

Evaluate & Communicate Findings

Track ongoing incidence to verify outbreak termination and publish final report.

1.3 Basic Epidemiologic and Health Measures

Prevalence vs Incidence

Prevalence ($P$): Total existing cases (old + new) divided by population at risk.

Incidence ($I$): Newly developed cases during a specified follow-up time.

Relationship: $P = I \times D$ (where $D$ is average disease duration).

1.4 Observational Study Designs: Case-Control Studies

Retrospective design starting with cases and controls. Measures exposure odds using the Odds Ratio ($OR = \frac{ad}{bc}$).

1.5 Observational Study Designs: Cohort Studies

Prospective design starting with healthy exposed and unexposed groups. Measures relative risk ($RR = \frac{I_e}{I_u}$) and attributable risk ($AR = I_e - I_u$).

1.6 Quantitative Methods in Health Screening

Sensitivity ($a/(a+c)$), Specificity ($d/(b+d)$), PPV ($a/(a+b)$), NPV ($d/(c+d)$).

1.7 Biases and Error Control in Epidemiologic Research

Selection Bias, Recall Bias, Attrition Bias, and Confounding control methods.

1.9 Chapter End Exercises and Self-Assessment Bank (Complete 75 Questions)

Part A: Multiple Choice Questions (20 MCQs)

1. Epidemiology is defined as the study of:
a) Individual patient treatments in hospital wards
b) Distribution and determinants of health states in human populations
c) Microscopic chemical interactions in blood
d) Surgical techniques for chronic diseases
Correct Answer: b — Epidemiology examines population-level health patterns and causes rather than bedside clinical care.
2. Point prevalence is defined as the proportion of a population with a disease at:
a) A single specific point in time
b) A 10-year follow-up interval
c) The time of birth only
d) The end of an epidemic period
Correct Answer: a — Point prevalence measures existing cases at one designated instant.
3. Incidence rate is defined as the rate at which:
a) Old cases recover from an illness
b) New cases develop in a population at risk
c) Hospital beds are occupied
d) Deaths occur among treated patients
Correct Answer: b — Incidence counts only newly occurring cases.
4. If a chronic disease cannot be cured but treatment extends patient survival, disease prevalence will:
a) Decrease to zero
b) Remain completely unchanged
c) Increase over time
d) Equal the incidence rate exactly
Correct Answer: c — Extending survival increases duration $D$; since $P = I \times D$, prevalence rises.
5. A case-control study is defined as an observational design that moves:
a) Forward from exposure to outcome
b) Retrospectively from outcome to past exposure
c) Forward from healthy status to recovery
d) Experimentally by giving drug doses
Correct Answer: b — Case-control studies start with disease status and look backward.
6. The primary measure of association calculated in a case-control study is the:
a) Relative risk
b) Odds ratio
c) Attributable risk
d) Population risk difference
Correct Answer: b — Because direct incidence cannot be measured, the odds ratio is the primary metric.
7. A cohort study is defined as a design that selects participants based on their:
a) Disease status
b) Exposure status
c) Hospital admission date
d) Recovery rate
Correct Answer: b — Cohort studies begin with healthy groups classified by exposure.
8. Relative risk is defined as the ratio of:
a) Odds of exposure in cases to odds in controls
b) Incidence rate in exposed group to incidence rate in unexposed group
c) Total deaths to total healthy individuals
d) Prevalence in urban areas to prevalence in rural areas
Correct Answer: b — $RR = I_e / I_u$.
9. An odds ratio equal to 1.0 means:
a) Exposure increases disease risk significantly
b) Exposure decreases disease risk significantly
c) There is no association between exposure and disease
d) Study calculations are mathematically invalid
Correct Answer: c — $OR = 1$ indicates equal exposure odds in cases and controls.
10. Case fatality rate is defined as the proportion of:
a) Deaths in a city divided by mid-year population
b) Diagnosed patients with a specific disease who die from it
c) Deaths caused by accidents in factory workers
d) Recovered cases relative to total admissions
Correct Answer: b — CFR = (deaths from disease / diagnosed cases) $\times 100$.
11. Sensitivity of a screening test is defined as the test's ability to correctly identify:
a) Healthy individuals as negative
b) Diseased individuals as positive
c) False positive cases in a lab
d) Carrier states in animals
Correct Answer: b — Sensitivity = $a/(a+c)$.
12. Specificity of a screening test is defined as the test's ability to correctly identify:
a) Diseased individuals as positive
b) Non-diseased individuals as negative
c) New incidence rates
d) Case fatality differences
Correct Answer: b — Specificity = $d/(b+d)$.
13. A false positive result in a screening program occurs when a person:
a) Has the disease and tests positive
b) Does not have the disease but tests positive
c) Has the disease but tests negative
d) Does not have the disease and tests negative
Correct Answer: b — A false positive is a healthy individual misclassified as positive.
14. Positive predictive value is defined as the proportion of:
a) Test-positive individuals who truly have the disease
b) Test-negative individuals who are healthy
c) Diseased people who test negative
d) Healthy people who test positive
Correct Answer: a — PPV = $a/(a+b)$.
15. Loss of study participants over time during a prospective study leads to:
a) Recall bias
b) Attrition bias
c) Selection bias at baseline
d) Interviewer bias
Correct Answer: b — Loss to follow-up creates attrition bias.
16. Which study design is most efficient for studying extremely rare diseases?
a) Cohort study
b) Case-control study
c) Randomised controlled trial
d) Field population survey
Correct Answer: b — Case-control studies start with pre-identified cases, making them ideal for rare diseases.
17. Crude death rate is traditionally expressed per:
a) 100 population
b) 1,000 population
c) 10,000 population
d) 100,000 population
Correct Answer: b — CDR is standardised per 1,000 mid-year population.
18. In a stable population, prevalence ($P$), incidence ($I$), and duration ($D$) are related as:
a) $P = I + D$
b) $P = I \times D$
c) $P = I / D$
d) $P = D / I$
Correct Answer: b — Prevalence equals incidence multiplied by average duration.
19. Which variable describes person-level characteristics in descriptive epidemiology?
a) Altitude
b) Rainfall
c) Age
d) Season
Correct Answer: c — Age is a fundamental person-level characteristic.
20. A screening test with very high sensitivity is preferred when:
a) Missing a true disease case has serious health consequences
b) Diagnostic treatment is very harmful
c) The test is extremely expensive
d) Disease is mild and self-healing
Correct Answer: a — High sensitivity minimises false negatives.

Part C: True / False Statements (20 Items)

1. Epidemiology focuses strictly on individual patient care in clinical settings. — False (Focuses on whole population groups).

2. Incidence rate includes both old and newly diagnosed cases in its numerator. — False (Includes only newly diagnosed cases).

3. A case-control study tracks participants forward into the future to see who gets sick. — False (Case-control studies are retrospective).

4. Relative risk can be calculated directly from a standard case-control study. — False (Yields odds ratios; direct incidence cannot be measured).

5. Fatal diseases with very short survival times tend to have low prevalence. — True (Short duration reduces prevalence because $P = I \times D$).

6. Specificity measures the test's ability to correctly clear non-diseased individuals. — True (Specificity = $d/(b+d)$).

7. False positive results cause unnecessary psychological anxiety for patients. — True.

8. An odds ratio of less than 1.0 suggests an exposure may protect against disease. — True ($OR < 1$ indicates protective factor).

9. Cohort studies require smaller sample sizes than case-control studies. — False (Cohort studies generally require larger samples).

10. Attributable risk measures the excess rate of disease caused directly by an exposure. — True ($AR = I_e - I_u$).

11. Case fatality rate is usually expressed per 100,000 mid-year population. — False (Expressed as a percentage of diagnosed cases).

12. Point prevalence measures disease status at one designated moment in time. — True.

13. Cohort study participants must already have the disease when follow-up starts. — False (Must be disease-free at baseline).

14. Positive predictive value changes depending on disease prevalence in the population. — True (PPV rises with prevalence).

15. Observational study designs involve giving new medicines to participants. — False (Observational studies only observe natural exposures).

16. Crude death rate accounts for age differences between different populations. — False (CDR does not adjust for age).

17. High sensitivity minimises the number of false negative test results. — True.

18. Loss of participants during cohort follow-up creates attrition bias. — True.

19. Retrospective studies examine historical records or past memories. — True.

20. A screening test provides a final medical diagnosis by itself. — False (Requires a gold standard test for final diagnosis).

Part E: Subjective & Analytical Questions (10 Detailed Solutions)

Q1. Definition & Objectives of Epidemiology

Solution: Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified human populations... Objectives: identify causes, measure disease burden, track trends, evaluate interventions, and guide public health policy.

Q2. Prevalence, Incidence & Duration

Solution: Prevalence ($P$) = existing cases / population; Incidence ($I$) = new cases / population at risk. In a stable population, $P = I \times D$. Long duration ($D$) leads to high prevalence even with moderate incidence.

Q3. Eight Key Steps of Outbreak Investigation

Solution: 1. Verify outbreak & diagnosis. 2. Define case & count cases. 3. Active case finding. 4. Descriptive analysis & epidemic curve. 5. Formulate hypothesis. 6. Analytical study testing ($OR/RR$). 7. Control measures. 8. Evaluate & communicate report.

Q4. Crude Death Rate vs Case Fatality Rate

Solution: CDR = (all deaths / mid-year population) $\times 1,000$. CFR = (deaths from specific disease / total diagnosed cases) $\times 100$. CDR evaluates population mortality; CFR evaluates disease lethality.

Q5. Case-Control Study Features

Solution: Retrospective study starting with cases and controls. Measures exposure odds using $OR = \frac{ad}{bc}$. Efficient for rare diseases, fast, low cost; vulnerable to recall/selection bias.

Q6. Cohort Study Features

Solution: Prospective study starting with healthy exposed and unexposed groups. Direct incidence $I_e = \frac{a}{a+b}, I_u = \frac{c}{c+d}$. Relative Risk $RR = \frac{I_e}{I_u}$, Attributable Risk $AR = I_e - I_u$.

Q7. Comparison Table: Case-Control vs Cohort

Solution: Direction (Retrospective vs Prospective), Selection (Outcome vs Exposure), Best for (Rare disease vs Rare exposure), Cost (Low vs High), Metric ($OR$ vs $RR, AR$).

Q8. Screening Metrics Definitions

Solution: Sensitivity = $\frac{a}{a+c}$, Specificity = $\frac{d}{b+d}$, PPV = $\frac{a}{a+b}$, NPV = $\frac{d}{c+d}$.

Q9. Screening Calculation Problem

Solution: Given $a=85, b=20, c=15, d=180$.
Sensitivity = $\frac{85}{100} \times 100 = 85\%$.
Specificity = $\frac{180}{200} \times 100 = 90\%$.
PPV = $\frac{85}{105} \times 100 = 80.95\%$.

Q10. Bias & Confounding

Solution: Bias = systematic study error. Confounding = third variable distorting exposure-outcome relationship. Recall bias = differential memory in cases vs controls. Attrition bias = differential loss to follow-up over time.
