Unit I: Epidemiology Fundamentals

Principles of epidemiological investigation, health measures, study designs & screening quantitative methods

1.1 Introduction to Epidemiology

Imagine that a single student in your college hostel develops a stomach upset. The doctor examines the student, gives oral rehydration and an antispasmodic, and the student recovers by evening. That is clinical medicine: one patient, one diagnosis, one treatment. But suppose that by the next afternoon, forty students living in Block-A of the same hostel develop vomiting and diarrhoea... This shift from caring for an individual to understanding the health experience of an entire group is the central purpose of epidemiology.
Formal Definition
Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified human populations, and the application of this study to the prevention and control of health problems.

Epidemiology vs Clinical Medicine

Aspect Clinical Medicine Epidemiology
Unit of concern Individual patient Population or community
Main question What is wrong with this patient? Why are people in this group getting sick?
Typical action Diagnose and treat Investigate, measure, and prevent

The Triad of Descriptive Epidemiology: Person, Place, Time

Person (Who?) — age, sex, occupation, immunisation status, diet.
Place (Where?) — village, hostel block, water source.
Time (When?) — onset dates, seasonal patterns, epidemic curves.

1.2 Principles of Epidemiologic Investigation

An epidemiologic investigation is a systematic, step-by-step procedure used to identify the source, cause, and mode of transmission of a disease within a population.

Sequential Protocol (8 Steps)
  1. Verify outbreak and diagnosis: Compare observed cases with baseline.
  2. Define case and count cases: Establish clinical, time, place criteria.
  3. Search for additional cases: Perform active door-to-door case finding.
  4. Perform descriptive analysis: Plot epidemic curve (person, place, time).
  5. Formulate working hypothesis: Propose plausible source/vehicle.
  6. Test and refine hypothesis: Run case-control or cohort analytical study.
  7. Implement control measures: Water chlorination, isolation, vaccination.
  8. Evaluate & communicate findings: Monitor ongoing cases & issue final report.

1.3 Basic Epidemiologic and Health Measures

Prevalence, Incidence & Disease Duration
$$ P = I \times D $$

Prevalence ($P$) equals Incidence ($I$) multiplied by average Duration ($D$).

Key Formulas Summary

Ratio: $\text{Ratio} = \frac{\text{Quantity A}}{\text{Quantity B}}$

Proportion: $\text{Proportion} = \frac{\text{Events in group}}{\text{Total in group}}$

Rate: $\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Events in period}}{\text{Population at risk}} \times k$

Attack Rate (AR): $\text{AR} = \frac{\text{Ill individuals}}{\text{Exposed at risk}} \times 100$

Case Fatality Rate (CFR): $\text{CFR} = \frac{\text{Deaths from disease}}{\text{Diagnosed cases}} \times 100$

1.4 Case-Control Studies

Definition
A case-control study is a retrospective analytical observational study that selects cases (diseased) and controls (healthy) and looks backward to assess past exposure.
Odds Ratio (OR) Formula
$$ OR = \frac{a/c}{b/d} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c} $$

1.5 Cohort Studies

Definition
A cohort study is a prospective/retrospective observational study that follows exposed ($a+b$) and unexposed ($c+d$) healthy groups forward in time to measure disease incidence directly.
Relative Risk & Attributable Risk
$$ RR = \frac{I_e}{I_u} = \frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)} $$ $$ AR = I_e - I_u, \quad AR\% = \frac{I_e - I_u}{I_e} \times 100 $$

1.6 Quantitative Methods in Health Screening

Screening Test Disease Present (Gold +) Disease Absent (Gold −)
Test Positive True Positive ($a$) False Positive ($b$)
Test Negative False Negative ($c$) True Negative ($d$)
Screening Metrics

Sensitivity: $\frac{a}{a+c} \times 100$

Specificity: $\frac{d}{b+d} \times 100$

Positive Predictive Value (PPV): $\frac{a}{a+b} \times 100$

Negative Predictive Value (NPV): $\frac{d}{c+d} \times 100$

1.7 Biases and Error Control in Epidemiologic Research

Selection Bias: Unrepresentative selection of cases/controls.
Recall Bias: Differential memory recall between sick cases and controls.
Attrition Bias: Differential loss to follow-up in cohort studies.
Confounding: Distortion by a third variable associated with exposure and outcome.

1.8 Solved Real-World Case Studies

Case Study 1: Mess Food Poisoning

60 ate paneer (36 ill), 40 did not (4 ill).
$I_e = 36/60 = 0.60$, $I_u = 4/40 = 0.10$.
$$ RR = \frac{0.60}{0.10} = 6.0 $$ Conclusion: Paneer consumers were 6 times as likely to become ill.

1.9 Chapter End Exercises and Self-Assessment Bank

Part A: Multiple Choice Questions (20 MCQs)

1. Epidemiology is defined as the study of:
a) Individual patient treatments
b) Distribution and determinants of health states in human populations
c) Microscopic chemical interactions
d) Surgical techniques
Correct Answer: b — Examines population-level health patterns and causes.
2. Point prevalence is defined as the proportion of a population with a disease at:
a) A single specific point in time
b) A 10-year follow-up interval
c) Birth only
d) End of epidemic
Correct Answer: a — Point prevalence measures existing cases at one instant.
3. Sensitivity of a screening test is computed as:
a) $a / (a+b)$
b) $a / (a+c)$
c) $d / (b+d)$
d) $d / (c+d)$
Correct Answer: b — Sensitivity = True Positives / Total True Diseased = $a / (a+c)$.

Part B: Numerical Practice Problems

Problem 1: Rapid Anaemia Screening

Out of 300 adults screened (100 diseased, 200 healthy), test gives 85 true positives and 20 false positives.
$\text{Sensitivity} = \frac{85}{100} \times 100 = 85\%$.
$\text{Specificity} = \frac{180}{200} \times 100 = 90\%$.
$\text{PPV} = \frac{85}{105} \times 100 = 80.95\%$.
