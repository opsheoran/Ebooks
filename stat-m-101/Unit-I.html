Unit I: Epidemiology Fundamentals

Principles of epidemiological investigation, health measures, study designs & screening quantitative methods

1.1 Introduction to Epidemiology

Imagine that a single student in your college hostel develops a stomach upset. The doctor examines the student, gives oral rehydration and an antispasmodic, and the student recovers by evening. That is clinical medicine: one patient, one diagnosis, one treatment. But suppose that by the next afternoon, forty students living in Block-A of the same hostel develop vomiting and diarrhoea, while students in Block-B remain perfectly well... This shift from caring for an individual to understanding the health experience of an entire group is the central purpose of epidemiology.
Formal Definition
Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified human populations, and the application of this study to the prevention and control of health problems.

Four Key Components

  1. Study: Systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data.
  2. Distribution: Pattern of disease by person, place, and time.
  3. Determinants: Causes, risk factors, or exposures that influence health outcomes.
  4. Application: Translating findings into community prevention and control.

Epidemiology vs Clinical Medicine

Aspect Clinical Medicine Epidemiology
Unit of concern Individual patient Population or community
Main question What is wrong with this patient? Why are people in this group getting sick?
Typical action Diagnose and treat Investigate, measure, and prevent
Example Treating one child with dengue Mapping a dengue cluster to remove breeding sites

The Triad of Descriptive Epidemiology: Person, Place, Time

Person (Who?) — age, sex, occupation, immunisation status, diet, and habits.

Place (Where?) — village, urban ward, hostel block, water source, or health facility.

Time (When?) — dates of onset, seasonal patterns, and epidemic curves.

1.2 Principles of Epidemiologic Investigation

Definition
An epidemiologic investigation is a systematic, step-by-step procedure used to identify the source, cause, and mode of transmission of a disease within a population, and to recommend control measures.
Sequential 8-Step Outbreak Protocol
  1. Step 1. Verify the outbreak and diagnosis: Confirm observed cases exceed baseline.
  2. Step 2. Define a case and count cases: Establish clinical, time, and place criteria.
  3. Step 3. Search for additional cases: Perform active door-to-door case finding.
  4. Step 4. Perform descriptive analysis: Plot epidemic curves by person, place, and time.
  5. Step 5. Formulate a working hypothesis: Propose plausible transmission vehicle.
  6. Step 6. Test and refine hypothesis: Execute analytical case-control or cohort studies.
  7. Step 7. Implement control measures: Water chlorination, isolation, vaccination.
  8. Step 8. Evaluate & communicate findings: Track ongoing incidence and publish final report.

1.3 Basic Epidemiologic and Health Measures

Prevalence, Incidence & Duration
$$ P = I \times D $$

Prevalence ($P$) = Incidence ($I$) $\times$ Average Duration ($D$).

Key Formulas

Ratio: $\text{Ratio} = \frac{\text{Quantity A}}{\text{Quantity B}}$

Proportion: $\text{Proportion} = \frac{\text{Events in group}}{\text{Total in group}}$

Rate: $\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Events in time period}}{\text{Population at risk}} \times k$

Attack Rate (AR): $\text{AR} = \frac{\text{Ill people}}{\text{Exposed at risk}} \times 100$

Case Fatality Rate (CFR): $\text{CFR} = \frac{\text{Deaths due to disease}}{\text{Diagnosed cases}} \times 100$

1.4 Observational Study Designs: Case-Control Studies

Definition
A case-control study is an observational retrospective analytical study that selects cases (diseased) and controls (healthy) and evaluates past exposure history.
Odds Ratio (OR)
$$ OR = \frac{a/c}{b/d} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c} $$

1.5 Observational Study Designs: Cohort Studies

Definition
A cohort study is an observational analytical study that selects healthy participants classified by exposure status and follows them forward in time to directly compute disease incidence.
Relative Risk (RR) & Attributable Risk (AR)
$$ RR = \frac{I_e}{I_u} = \frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)} $$ $$ AR = I_e - I_u, \quad AR\% = \frac{I_e - I_u}{I_e} \times 100 $$

1.6 Quantitative Methods in Health Screening

Screening Test Disease Present (Gold +) Disease Absent (Gold −)
Test Positive True Positive ($a$) False Positive ($b$)
Test Negative False Negative ($c$) True Negative ($d$)
Screening Indicators

Sensitivity: $\frac{a}{a+c} \times 100$

Specificity: $\frac{d}{b+d} \times 100$

Positive Predictive Value (PPV): $\frac{a}{a+b} \times 100$

Negative Predictive Value (NPV): $\frac{d}{c+d} \times 100$

1.7 Biases and Error Control in Epidemiologic Research

Selection Bias: Unrepresentative selection of participants.

Recall Bias: Differential recall of past exposure between sick cases and controls.

Attrition Bias: Differential loss of participants over time in cohort follow-up.

Confounding: Distortion produced by a third variable associated with exposure and outcome.

1.8 Solved Real-World Case Studies

Case Study 1: Hostel Mess Food Poisoning

60 ate paneer (36 ill), 40 did not eat paneer (4 ill).
$I_e = 36/60 = 0.60$, $I_u = 4/40 = 0.10$.
$$ RR = \frac{0.60}{0.10} = 6.0 $$ Conclusion: Students eating paneer were 6 times as likely to become ill.

Case Study 2: Smoking & Chronic Cough

200 smokers (20 cough), 300 non-smokers (6 cough).
$I_e = 0.10$, $I_u = 0.02$. $RR = 5.0$, $AR = 0.08$, $AR\% = 80\%$.

1.9 Chapter End Exercises and Self-Assessment Bank

Part A: Multiple Choice Questions (20 MCQs)

1. Epidemiology is defined as the study of:
a) Individual patient treatments in hospital wards
b) Distribution and determinants of health states in human populations
c) Microscopic chemical interactions in blood
d) Surgical techniques for chronic diseases
Correct Answer: b — Epidemiology examines population-level health patterns and causes rather than bedside clinical care.
2. Point prevalence is defined as the proportion of a population with a disease at:
a) A single specific point in time
b) A 10-year follow-up interval
c) The time of birth only
d) The end of an epidemic period
Correct Answer: a — Point prevalence measures existing cases at one designated instant.
3. Incidence rate is defined as the rate at which:
a) Old cases recover from an illness
b) New cases develop in a population at risk
c) Hospital beds are occupied
d) Deaths occur among treated patients
Correct Answer: b — Incidence counts only newly occurring cases.
4. If a chronic disease cannot be cured but treatment extends survival, prevalence will:
a) Decrease to zero
b) Remain completely unchanged
c) Increase over time
d) Equal incidence rate exactly
Correct Answer: c — Extending duration $D$ increases prevalence because $P = I \times D$.
5. A case-control study is defined as an observational design that moves:
a) Forward from exposure to outcome
b) Retrospectively from outcome to past exposure
c) Forward from healthy status to recovery
d) Experimentally by giving drug doses
Correct Answer: b — Case-control studies start with disease status and look backward.
