Unit I: Epidemiology Fundamentals

Principles of epidemiological investigation, health measures, study designs & screening quantitative methods

1.1 Introduction to Epidemiology

Imagine that a single student in your college hostel develops a stomach upset. The doctor examines the student, gives oral rehydration and an antispasmodic, and the student recovers by evening. That is clinical medicine: one patient, one diagnosis, one treatment. But suppose that by the next afternoon, forty students living in Block-A of the same hostel develop vomiting and diarrhoea, while students in Block-B remain perfectly well. Treating each of the forty students is necessary, yet it is not sufficient. Someone must ask why Block-A alone was affected, what was served at breakfast, whether the milk was left uncovered, and how the contaminated food reached only one section of the dining hall. This shift from caring for an individual to understanding the health experience of an entire group is the central purpose of epidemiology.
Formal Definition
Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified human populations, and the application of this study to the prevention and control of health problems.

Four Key Components

  1. Study: Systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data.
  2. Distribution: Pattern of disease by person, place, and time.
  3. Determinants: Causes, risk factors, or exposures that influence health outcomes.
  4. Application: Translating findings into community prevention and control.

Epidemiology vs Clinical Medicine

Aspect Clinical Medicine Epidemiology
Unit of concern Individual patient Population or community
Main question What is wrong with this patient? Why are people in this group getting sick?
Typical action Diagnose and treat Investigate, measure, and prevent
Example Treating one child with dengue Mapping a dengue cluster to remove breeding sites

1.2 Principles of Epidemiologic Investigation: Sequential 8-Step Outbreak Protocol

When several people in a village fall ill with similar symptoms after a community feast, panic spreads quickly. Neighbours blame the weather, the water, or a particular dish. A health worker cannot afford to guess. She must follow a clear, step-by-step method to confirm that an outbreak is truly occurring, find every affected person, describe the pattern of illness, form a sensible explanation, test it with data, stop further cases, and tell the community what happened. This systematic procedure is called an epidemiologic outbreak investigation.
Step 1

Verify the Outbreak and Diagnosis

Goal: Confirm that an actual outbreak exists and verify the medical accuracy of reported diagnoses.

Explanation: An outbreak is confirmed when the observed number of cases significantly exceeds the expected baseline number for that specific geographic location and time of year. Epidemiologists review medical charts and run laboratory tests (such as blood cultures, stool PCR, or serology) to ensure that the reported illness is not a false alarm caused by diagnostic error or changes in local reporting habits.

Real-World Example: A rural Primary Health Centre (PHC) normally sees 2 cases of typhoid per month. In the first week of August, 15 patients present with high fever and positive Widal/blood cultures. Because 15 cases far exceed the baseline of 2, an outbreak is verified.

Step 2

Define a Case and Count Cases

Goal: Create a standard, objective "Case Definition" to decide exactly who should be counted in the investigation.

Explanation: A case definition is a set of standard criteria that includes clinical symptoms (e.g. fever $> 38.5^\circ\text{C}$), laboratory confirmation, and restrictions on time, place, and person. Cases are categorized into Confirmed (lab-proven), Probable (typical clinical features without lab tests), and Suspected (mild or early symptoms). Fixing the definition early prevents misclassification bias.

Real-World Example: For a dengue outbreak in a college town, the case definition is: "Any resident of Hostel Block-A presenting between 1 August and 15 August with acute fever, joint pain, and a positive NS1 antigen test or platelet count $< 100,000/\mu\text{L}$."

Step 3

Search for Additional Cases (Active Case Finding)

Goal: Find every affected individual in the community, including mild or unreported cases.

Explanation: Relying only on patients who visit hospitals (passive reporting) misses mild cases or people living far from clinics. Epidemiologists conduct active door-to-door field surveys, review health registry logs, and contact local schools and factories. Finding all cases ensures an accurate attack rate calculation.

Real-World Example: During a gastroenteritis outbreak, health teams visit every household in the village, finding 25 children with mild diarrhoea who were treated at home with ORS and never registered at the district hospital.

Step 4

Perform Descriptive Analysis & Plot Epidemic Curves

Goal: Organize data by Person, Place, and Time to uncover outbreak characteristics.

Explanation: Cases are summarized by age, sex, occupation (Person), mapped on spot maps to identify geographical clusters (Place), and plotted on an Epidemic Curve (a histogram of case counts by date of symptom onset). The shape of the epidemic curve reveals the nature of transmission:

  • Point-Source Outbreak: Sharp, steep rise and rapid fall within one incubation period (e.g. contaminated food dish at a wedding).
  • Continuous Common-Source: Broad, flat plateau (e.g. ongoing sewage leakage into a municipal water main).
  • Propagated Outbreak: Series of progressively higher peaks spaced one incubation period apart (e.g. person-to-person spread of measles or influenza).
Step 5

Formulate a Working Hypothesis

Goal: Propose a plausible explanation for the source, mode of transmission, and specific vehicle of the disease.

Explanation: By analyzing the descriptive data from Step 4, investigators form an educated hypothesis. For example, if spot mapping shows 90% of cases live along one pipeline, or if food histories reveal all sick students ate cold paneer at lunch, that specific vehicle becomes the working hypothesis.

Real-World Example: In a college hostel, 36 out of 40 sick students ate the paneer dish served on Monday, while non-sick students ate only dal and rice. Contaminated paneer is hypothesized as the source vehicle.

Step 6

Test and Refine Hypothesis (Analytical Studies)

Goal: Quantify the statistical association between the suspected exposure and the disease using formal study designs.

Explanation: Investigators execute a rapid Case-Control study (comparing past exposures of sick cases vs healthy controls) or a Cohort study (comparing disease attack rates in exposed vs unexposed groups). They compute mathematical measures of risk such as Odds Ratios ($OR$), Relative Risk ($RR$), and Attack Rates ($AR$). Laboratory testing of food/water samples is also conducted to confirm the pathogen.

Real-World Example: Computing an Attack Rate Ratio $RR = \frac{60\% \text{ attack rate in paneer eaters}}{10\% \text{ attack rate in non-eaters}} = 6.0$. This statistically confirms that paneer eaters had 6 times higher risk of illness.

Step 7

Implement Control and Prevention Measures

Goal: Take immediate action to stop further disease transmission and protect the public.

Explanation: Control measures must be initiated as soon as a strong hypothesis is established—without waiting for final lab confirmation. Interventions target the source (e.g. closing a contaminated food vendor, chlorinating water tanks), interrupt transmission (e.g. vector control spraying for mosquitoes), or protect susceptible hosts (e.g. post-exposure vaccination, ring immunization, or prophylactic antibiotics).

Real-World Example: Immediately shutting down the implicated hostel mess, chlorinating the overhead water reservoir, and providing oral rehydration salts to all affected students.

Step 8

Evaluate Control Measures and Communicate Findings

Goal: Monitor ongoing disease incidence to verify control measures are effective, and publish an official outbreak report.

Explanation: The epidemiology team continues daily active surveillance. If the epidemic curve drops back to baseline, the intervention was successful. A detailed final report is presented to health authorities, state ministries, and the public to update safety policies and prevent similar future outbreaks.

Real-World Example: Tracking zero new cases for two incubation periods (14 days), documenting proper food handling protocols, and submitting the final report to the University Health Officer and State Directorate of Health Services.

1.3 Basic Epidemiologic and Health Measures

Prevalence vs Incidence

Prevalence ($P$): Total existing cases (old + new) divided by population at risk at a point/period in time.

Incidence ($I$): Newly developed cases during a specified follow-up time divided by population at risk.

Bathtub Model: $P = I \times D$
Prevalence ($P$) is like water level in a bathtub. Incidence ($I$) is water flowing in from the tap. Duration ($D$, time to recovery or death) is water draining out. Chronic diseases (long $D$) keep prevalence high even if annual incidence is low.

1.4 Observational Study Designs: Case-Control Studies

Retrospective analytical study starting with diseased cases and healthy controls to compare past exposure odds.

$$\text{Odds Ratio } (OR) = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c}$$

1.5 Observational Study Designs: Cohort Studies

Prospective analytical study starting with healthy exposed and unexposed groups followed forward in time to compute direct disease incidence.

$$\text{Relative Risk } (RR) = \frac{I_e}{I_u} = \frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)}$$

1.6 Quantitative Methods in Health Screening

Sensitivity: Proportion of true diseased who test positive ($a / (a+c)$).

Specificity: Proportion of true non-diseased who test negative ($d / (b+d)$).

PPV: Proportion of test positives who actually have disease ($a / (a+b)$).

NPV: Proportion of test negatives who are truly healthy ($d / (c+d)$).

1.7 Biases and Error Control in Epidemiologic Research

Systematic errors including Selection Bias, Recall Bias, Attrition Bias, and Confounding distorting exposure-outcome relationships.

1.9 Chapter End Exercises and Self-Assessment Bank

Part A: Multiple Choice Questions (20 MCQs)

1. In an outbreak investigation, Step 1 is to:
a) Immediately publish the final report
b) Verify the outbreak and laboratory diagnosis
c) Vaccinate the whole state
d) Close all schools permanently
Correct Answer: b — Step 1 verifies that cases exceed baseline and confirms diagnosis.
2. An epidemic curve showing a sharp rise and rapid fall within one incubation period indicates a:
a) Propagated outbreak
b) Point-source outbreak
c) Continuous endemic baseline
d) Person-to-person viral spread
Correct Answer: b — Point-source outbreaks stem from a single simultaneous exposure event.
