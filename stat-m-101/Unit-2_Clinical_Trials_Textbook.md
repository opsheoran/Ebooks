# 2.1 Introduction to Clinical Trials

> When a doctor gives a patient a tablet for fever, the doctor knows the drug has already been tested. But before that tablet reached the market, someone had to answer important questions. Does the drug work? Is it safe? What is the right dose? These questions are answered through clinical trials. A clinical trial is a study done with human beings to test a new medicine, device, vaccine, or treatment method. It is the bridge between laboratory research and everyday medical use. In India, clinical trials help find better treatments for diseases like tuberculosis, diabetes, cancer, and heart disease. They also help us compare a new treatment with the one already in use. Without trials, doctors would have no solid proof that a treatment is helpful or safe.

## What is a Clinical Trial?

A **clinical trial** is a research study carried out on human volunteers. It tests how safe and useful a new treatment, drug, vaccine, or medical device is. The treatment being tested is called the **investigational product**.

*Example:* A company makes a new cough syrup. Before it can be sold in pharmacies, it must be tested on volunteers in a clinical trial. Researchers check whether the syrup reduces cough better than the existing product and whether it causes any side effects.

## Why Do We Need Clinical Trials?

We need clinical trials because laboratory tests on animals or cells cannot tell us everything about how a treatment will work in humans. Trials give us proof about:

- **Safety:** Does the treatment cause harm?
- **Effectiveness:** Does the treatment produce the desired result?
- **Dose:** How much should be given?
- **Comparison:** Is the new treatment better than the old one?

*Example:* During the COVID-19 pandemic, clinical trials were used to find out which vaccines protected people from severe disease. These trials gave governments the confidence to approve vaccines for public use.

## Key Terms

- **Participant or subject:** The person who volunteers to take part in the trial.
- **Investigator:** The doctor or researcher who runs the trial.
- **Sponsor:** The organization that pays for the trial. It can be a government agency, a university, or a company.
- **Protocol:** A detailed written plan that describes how the trial will be done.

# 2.2 Classification of Clinical Trials

> Not all clinical trials are the same. Some test new drugs. Some test vaccines. Some try to prevent disease, while others try to diagnose it. Trials can also differ in how they are designed. Some compare a new drug with a sugar pill. Others compare it with the best existing treatment. Understanding these types helps us read trial results correctly. It also helps doctors and patients choose the right treatment.

## By Purpose or Objective

**Treatment trials:** These test new treatments, drugs, or combinations in people who already have a disease.

*Example:* A trial testing a new chemotherapy drug in cancer patients.

**Prevention trials:** These test ways to prevent disease in people who do not have it.

*Example:* A trial testing whether a vitamin supplement reduces the risk of anaemia in adolescent girls.

**Diagnostic trials:** These test new methods or tools to find a disease.

*Example:* A trial comparing a new blood test with an X-ray for detecting tuberculosis.

**Screening trials:** These test ways to find a disease early, before symptoms appear.

*Example:* A trial checking whether mobile breast-cancer screening camps save lives in rural areas.

**Quality-of-life trials:** These test ways to improve comfort and daily life for people with chronic illness.

*Example:* A trial testing whether yoga reduces pain and tiredness in patients with arthritis.

## By Control Group

**Placebo-controlled trials:** One group gets the new treatment. The other group gets a **placebo**, which looks like the treatment but has no active medicine.

*Example:* One group gets a new headache tablet. The other group gets a tablet that looks the same but contains no drug.

**Active-controlled trials:** The new treatment is compared with the best standard treatment already in use.

*Example:* A new diabetes drug is compared with metformin, which is already widely used.

## By Blinding

**Open-label trial:** Both the participant and the researcher know which treatment is being given.

**Single-blind trial:** Only the researcher knows which treatment the participant is getting.

**Double-blind trial:** Neither the participant nor the researcher knows who is getting the new drug and who is getting the placebo. This is the most common design because it reduces bias.

*Example:* In a double-blind trial of a new painkiller, the pills for both groups look identical. Codes are used so that no one knows the group assignment until the trial ends.

# 2.3 Phases of Clinical Trials

> A new drug is not tested on thousands of people on the first day. Testing happens step by step. Each step is called a phase. Early phases use a small number of people and focus on safety. Later phases use more people and focus on whether the drug really works. If a drug passes all phases, it can be approved for public use. Even after approval, experts keep watching it. This organised step-by-step process protects volunteers and gives trustworthy results.

![Diagram 1: Phases of a Clinical Trial](images/unit2_diagram1_phases.svg)

*Figure 2.1: Phases of clinical trials from early safety testing to post-marketing surveillance.*

## Phase 0

Phase 0 is a very early stage. A small dose of the drug is given to a few people. Researchers want to see how the drug behaves in the human body. This phase does not test whether the drug treats the disease.

*Example:* A new cancer drug is given to 10 patients at a very low dose to study how it moves in the blood.

## Phase I

Phase I trials test **safety** and **dose**. They usually include 20 to 100 healthy volunteers or patients. Researchers find out the safe dose range and watch for side effects.

*Example:* A new malaria vaccine is given to healthy adults in increasing doses to find the highest safe dose.

## Phase II

Phase II trials test whether the drug **works** and give more safety information. They usually include 100 to 300 patients with the disease.

*Example:* A new asthma inhaler is tested on 200 asthma patients to see if it improves breathing.

## Phase III

Phase III trials compare the new treatment with the standard treatment or placebo. They usually include 1,000 to 5,000 patients. These trials provide the main evidence for approval by drug regulators.

*Example:* A new heart medicine is tested in 3,000 patients across many hospitals to see if it reduces heart attacks better than the current medicine.

## Phase IV

Phase IV trials happen after the drug is already approved and sold. They look at long-term safety, rare side effects, and how the drug works in everyday practice.

*Example:* After a new painkiller is sold, doctors report any unexpected side effects in patients who use it regularly.

## Summary of Phases

| Phase | Main Question | Typical Number of Participants | Main Focus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phase 0 | How does the drug act in the body? | Very few | Early behaviour |
| Phase I | Is it safe? What dose can be given? | 20–100 | Safety and dose |
| Phase II | Does it work? | 100–300 | Effectiveness and short-term safety |
| Phase III | Is it better than standard care? | 1,000–5,000 | Large-scale comparison |
| Phase IV | What happens after long-term use? | Large numbers | Long-term safety and real-world use |

# 2.4 Randomized Controlled Trials

> Suppose a doctor believes that a new herbal tea lowers blood pressure. If she gives the tea only to patients she likes, her personal belief may affect the result. To avoid this, researchers use a special design called the randomized controlled trial, or RCT. In an RCT, participants are divided into groups by chance, like tossing a coin. One group gets the new treatment. The other group gets a placebo or the standard treatment. Then both groups are followed in the same way. Because the groups are formed by chance, they are likely to be similar at the start. Any difference in outcome at the end can then be credited to the treatment.

## What is an RCT?

A **randomized controlled trial** is a type of clinical trial in which participants are assigned to treatment groups by chance. One group receives the new treatment. The other group, called the **control group**, receives a placebo or standard treatment. The results of the two groups are then compared.

![Diagram 2: Design of a Randomized Controlled Trial](images/unit2_diagram2_rct_design.svg)

*Figure 2.2: Basic steps in a randomized controlled trial.*

## Randomization

**Randomization** means allocating participants to groups using a method based on chance. It can be done with a computer, a random-number table, or sealed envelopes.

Randomization is important because it:

- makes the groups similar at the start;
- balances known and unknown factors such as age, sex, and disease severity;
- reduces selection bias; and
- allows the use of standard statistical tests.

*Example:* In a trial of a new fertilizer for crops, randomization makes sure that fertile plots are not all placed in the treatment group. The same idea applies to patients.

## Control Group

The **control group** is the group that does not receive the new treatment. It may receive a placebo, no treatment, or the standard treatment. The control group acts as a comparison. Without it, we cannot know if the new treatment is truly better.

*Example:* If 70% of patients in the new-drug group recover and 65% in the control group recover, the real benefit may be small. The control group helps us see this clearly.

## Placebo

A **placebo** is a dummy treatment that looks like the real treatment but has no active ingredient. It is used to separate the true effect of the drug from the psychological effect of receiving a treatment.

*Example:* A patient given a sugar pill for headache may feel better simply because he believes he is being treated. This is called the placebo effect.

## Blinding or Masking

**Blinding** means hiding the treatment assignment from one or more people involved in the trial.

- In a **single-blind** trial, only the participant does not know the treatment.
- In a **double-blind** trial, neither the participant nor the investigator knows the treatment.
- In a **triple-blind** trial, the participant, investigator, and data analysts all do not know the treatment.

Blinding reduces bias from expectations. If a doctor knows a patient is getting a new drug, she may look harder for improvement.

## Allocation Concealment

**Allocation concealment** means keeping the upcoming group assignment hidden until the participant is ready to be enrolled. This prevents the investigator from choosing which participant goes into which group.

*Example:* Sealed opaque envelopes or a central telephone randomization system are used so that the doctor cannot guess the next allocation.

## Advantages of RCTs

- They provide strong evidence about cause and effect.
- Randomization reduces bias.
- Blinding reduces the influence of expectations.
- They are accepted as the gold standard for testing treatments.

## Limitations of RCTs

- They can be expensive and time-consuming.
- Results may not apply to all real-world patients.
- Some participants may drop out.
- Ethical issues can arise, especially when a placebo is used for serious diseases.

# 2.5 Ethics in Clinical Trials

> Clinical trials involve real people. These people may be sick, poor, or unable to fully understand the risks. That is why ethics are at the heart of every trial. No matter how promising a new drug looks, it cannot be tested on humans unless ethical rules are followed. The safety and rights of participants must always come before the interests of science or business. In India, ethics committees review every trial. They check the protocol, the consent form, and the balance between possible benefit and possible harm.

## Informed Consent

**Informed consent** means that a participant agrees to take part in a trial after understanding its purpose, procedures, risks, benefits, and rights. The consent must be given freely without force or pressure.

The consent form should be in a language the participant understands. For people who cannot read, the form is read out and a thumb impression is taken.

*Example:* Before joining a cancer drug trial, a patient is told that the drug may shrink the tumour but may also cause nausea or low blood count. The patient then decides whether to join.

## Ethics Committee or Institutional Review Board

Every clinical trial must be approved by an **ethics committee** or **institutional review board**. This group includes doctors, scientists, lawyers, and community members. It reviews the trial to protect participants.

The committee checks:

- whether the trial is scientifically sound;
- whether the risks are reasonable compared with the benefits;
- whether informed consent is proper; and
- whether vulnerable participants are protected.

## Vulnerable Populations

Some people need extra protection. These include children, pregnant women, prisoners, mentally ill persons, and economically disadvantaged groups. Trials in these groups need stronger justification.

*Example:* A trial on a new vaccine for children must first show safety in adults. Only then can it be tested in children, and at lower doses.

## Benefit-Risk Balance

A trial should offer a fair balance. The possible benefit to the participant or to society should be greater than the risk. If a new drug is very risky and offers little benefit, the trial should not be done.

*Example:* Testing a completely untested chemical on healthy volunteers for a minor skin cream is not ethical. The risk is too high for a small benefit.

## Key Ethical Principles

The main principles come from the **Declaration of Helsinki** and Indian Council of Medical Research guidelines:

1. **Respect for persons:** Treat participants with dignity and respect their choices.
2. **Beneficence:** Do good and maximise possible benefits.
3. **Non-maleficence:** Do no harm and minimise risks.
4. **Justice:** Share the benefits and burdens fairly. Do not exploit poor or helpless groups.

## Conflict of Interest

A **conflict of interest** happens when a researcher or sponsor may gain money or fame from the trial results. This should be declared so that it does not affect the trial.

# 2.6 Multi-Centre Trials

> A single hospital may not have enough patients for a large trial. It may also see only one type of patient. To solve this, researchers run the same trial in many hospitals or clinics at the same time. This is called a multi-centre trial. These trials are common for diseases like cancer, diabetes, and heart disease, where thousands of patients are needed. Multi-centre trials can also include patients from different regions or countries, so the results apply to a wider population.

## What is a Multi-Centre Trial?

A **multi-centre trial** is a clinical trial conducted at more than one hospital, clinic, or research site at the same time. All sites follow the same protocol.

*Example:* A trial of a new stroke medicine may run in 50 hospitals across India. Each hospital follows the same rules for selecting patients, giving treatment, and recording results.

## Why Use Multi-Centre Trials?

- They allow faster recruitment of large numbers of participants.
- They include patients from different backgrounds and regions.
- They make the results more general.
- They can compare how the treatment works in different settings.

## Challenges of Multi-Centre Trials

- **Standardisation:** Each site must follow the protocol exactly. Training is needed.
- **Communication:** Investigators at different sites must share information regularly.
- **Quality control:** Data from all sites must be checked for accuracy.
- **Ethics approval:** Each site may need approval from its own ethics committee.
- **Cultural differences:** Informed consent and patient care may need local adaptation.

## Data Safety and Monitoring Board

Large trials often have a **data safety and monitoring board**. This is an independent group that looks at the trial data while it is running. It can stop the trial early if the new treatment is clearly harmful or clearly much better than the control.

*Example:* During a vaccine trial, the monitoring board may stop the trial early if the vaccine proves highly effective before the planned end date.

# 2.7 Sample Size Estimation

> Suppose you want to know if a new teaching method improves exam scores. If you test it on only two students, the result may be due to chance. If you test it on two hundred students, the result is more trustworthy. The same idea applies to clinical trials. The number of participants in a trial is called the sample size. Choosing the right sample size is important. Too few participants and the trial may miss a real benefit. Too many participants wastes money and puts extra people at risk. Sample size estimation is the process of deciding how many people are needed.

## Why is Sample Size Important?

The sample size affects whether the trial can give a reliable answer. A trial that is too small may fail to detect a useful treatment. A trial that is too large is wasteful and unethical because it exposes more people than necessary to risk.

## Factors Affecting Sample Size

Several factors influence the sample size:

1. **Significance level (alpha):** This is the chance of saying a treatment works when it actually does not. It is usually set at 5%, or 0.05. A lower alpha needs a larger sample.

2. **Power (1 minus beta):** This is the chance of finding a real effect if it exists. It is usually set at 80% or 90%. Higher power needs a larger sample.

3. **Effect size:** This is the difference between treatments that the trial wants to detect. A smaller effect needs a larger sample.

4. **Variability:** If the outcome varies a lot between people, the sample size must be larger.

5. **Dropout rate:** Some participants may leave the trial early. The sample size is increased to account for this.

## Basic Idea of Sample Size Formula

The sample size formula depends on the type of outcome. For comparing two proportions, a simple form is:

$$
n = \frac{(Z_{\alpha/2} + Z_{\beta})^2 \times 2p(1-p)}{(p_1 - p_2)^2}
$$

where:

- $n$ = sample size per group;
- $Z_{\alpha/2}$ = standard normal value for the chosen alpha;
- $Z_{\beta}$ = standard normal value for the chosen beta;
- $p_1$ and $p_2$ = expected proportions in the two groups;
- $p$ = average of $p_1$ and $p_2$.

In practice, statisticians use software to calculate sample size. The important thing is to understand the factors involved.

*Example:* Suppose a new drug is expected to cure 70% of patients, while the old drug cures 50%. To detect this difference with 80% power and 5% significance, the trial may need about 80 patients in each group.

# 2.8 Solved Examples

## Example 1: Identifying the Phase

A company wants to test a new malaria drug in 30 healthy adults to find the safe dose range. Which phase is this?

**Answer:** This is a **Phase I** trial because the main aim is safety and dose finding in a small group.

## Example 2: Understanding Randomization

In a trial of a new vitamin D supplement for bone health, 200 women are split into two groups by computer-generated random numbers. Group A gets the supplement. Group B gets a tablet that looks the same but has no active ingredient. Why is randomization used?

**Answer:** Randomization makes the two groups similar at the start. It balances age, diet, and other factors that could affect bone health. This way, any difference in bone density at the end is more likely due to the supplement.

## Example 3: Blinding in a Trial

A trial tests a new skin cream for eczema. The doctor knows which patients get the real cream and which get the placebo. The patients do not know. What type of blinding is this?

**Answer:** This is a **single-blind** trial because only the participants are blinded. The investigator knows the treatment assignment.

## Example 4: Sample Size Reasoning

A researcher plans a trial to compare a new blood-pressure tablet with the standard tablet. She expects a difference of 5 mmHg between the groups. Previous studies show a standard deviation of 10 mmHg. Why might she need a large sample?

**Answer:** The expected difference is small compared with the variation in blood pressure. A larger sample is needed to detect a small difference reliably.

## Example 5: Ethics Check

A trial wants to test a new acne cream on 12-year-old school children. What special issue must be considered?

**Answer:** Children are a vulnerable group. The trial must first show safety in adults. Informed consent must be taken from parents, and the children should also agree in a way they understand. The ethics committee must give special approval.

## Part E: Subjective and Analytical Questions (10 Questions with Detailed Solutions)

### Q1. What is a clinical trial? Why is it important?

**Solution:**

A clinical trial is a research study done with human volunteers to test a new treatment, drug, vaccine, or medical device. It is important because it gives proof about safety and effectiveness before a treatment is approved for public use. Without trials, doctors would not know whether a new treatment really helps or causes harm.

### Q2. Describe the different phases of clinical trials.

**Solution:**

- **Phase 0:** Very small study to see how the drug acts in the body.
- **Phase I:** Tests safety and dose in 20–100 healthy volunteers or patients.
- **Phase II:** Tests effectiveness and short-term safety in 100–300 patients.
- **Phase III:** Compares the new treatment with standard care in 1,000–5,000 patients.
- **Phase IV:** Monitors long-term safety and real-world use after the drug is approved.

### Q3. What is randomization? Why is it used in RCTs?

**Solution:**

Randomization is the process of assigning participants to treatment groups by chance. It is used to make the groups similar at the start of the trial. This balances known and unknown factors and reduces selection bias. It also allows researchers to use standard statistical tests to compare the groups.

### Q4. Explain the difference between a placebo and a control group.

**Solution:**

A **control group** is the group that does not receive the new treatment. It may receive a placebo, standard treatment, or no treatment. A **placebo** is a dummy treatment that looks like the real treatment but has no active ingredient. The placebo is often used as the control in drug trials to separate the true drug effect from the psychological effect of receiving treatment.

### Q5. What is blinding? Describe single-blind and double-blind trials.

**Solution:**

Blinding means hiding the treatment assignment from people involved in the trial.

- **Single-blind:** The participant does not know the treatment, but the investigator knows.
- **Double-blind:** Neither the participant nor the investigator knows the treatment.

Blinding reduces bias caused by expectations. It makes the trial results more reliable.

### Q6. What is informed consent? Why is it important?

**Solution:**

Informed consent means that a participant agrees to take part in a trial after understanding its purpose, procedures, possible risks, possible benefits, and rights. It must be given freely without pressure. It is important because it respects the participant's right to choose and protects them from harm.

### Q7. List the main ethical principles that guide clinical trials.

**Solution:**

The main ethical principles are:

1. **Respect for persons:** Treat participants with dignity and respect their decisions.
2. **Beneficence:** Act in the best interest of participants and maximise benefits.
3. **Non-maleficence:** Do no harm and minimise risks.
4. **Justice:** Share the benefits and burdens fairly. Do not exploit vulnerable groups.

### Q8. What is a multi-centre trial? Give its advantages and challenges.

**Solution:**

A multi-centre trial is a clinical trial conducted at more than one hospital or site at the same time, following the same protocol.

**Advantages:**
- faster recruitment of participants;
- inclusion of people from different regions;
- results that apply to a wider population.

**Challenges:**
- need for standard training across sites;
- communication between sites;
- quality control of data;
- ethics approval at each site;
- possible cultural differences.

### Q9. Why is sample size estimation important in clinical trials? What factors affect it?

**Solution:**

Sample size estimation is important because a trial that is too small may miss a real treatment effect, while a trial that is too large wastes resources and exposes extra people to risk.

The main factors that affect sample size are:

- significance level (alpha);
- statistical power (1 minus beta);
- effect size;
- variability of the outcome; and
- expected dropout rate.

### Q10. A new drug for diabetes is expected to reduce blood sugar by 20 mg/dL more than the standard drug. The standard deviation is 30 mg/dL. Will this trial need a small or large sample? Why?

**Solution:**

This trial will likely need a large sample. The expected difference of 20 mg/dL is small compared with the standard deviation of 30 mg/dL. When the effect size is small relative to variability, more participants are needed to detect a true difference with reasonable confidence and power.

---

# 2.10 Summary

Clinical trials are research studies done on human volunteers to test new treatments. They provide proof of safety and effectiveness before a drug or device is approved.

Trials can be classified by purpose, control group, and blinding. The main phases are Phase 0, I, II, III, and IV. Early phases focus on safety and dose. Later phases focus on effectiveness, comparison, and long-term monitoring.

Randomized controlled trials are the strongest design. Randomization creates similar groups. Control groups and placebos allow fair comparison. Blinding reduces bias from expectations.

Ethics protect participants. Informed consent, ethics committee approval, and special protection for vulnerable groups are essential. Multi-centre trials help recruit more participants and make results more general.

Sample size must be planned carefully. It depends on alpha, power, effect size, variability, and dropout rate.
