# 3.1 Introduction to Research Design

> Suppose your teacher asks you to find out how many students in your college use public transport. You cannot simply guess. You need a plan. You must decide whom to ask, how many students to include, what questions to ask, and how to record the answers. This plan is called a research design. A good research design is like a road map. It tells you where to start, what to do, and how to reach your goal. Without a design, your study may become messy. You may collect wrong data, ask the wrong people, or forget important details. In this unit, we learn how to plan a study, collect data, and prepare it for analysis.

## What is Research Design?

A **research design** is the plan or blue print of a study. It describes how the research will be carried out. It answers questions like:

- What do we want to study?
- Who will be studied?
- How will data be collected?
- How will data be analysed?

*Example:* If you want to study the eating habits of college students, your design will say how many students you will survey, what questions you will ask, and how you will record their answers.

## Why Do We Need a Research Design?

A research design is needed because it:

- keeps the study focused and organised;
- saves time and money;
- helps collect useful and correct data;
- makes it easier to analyse results; and
- helps others understand and repeat the study.

*Example:* A farmer wants to test which fertiliser gives more yield. Without a design, he might use different amounts on different soils. With a design, he can compare the fertilisers fairly.

## Basic Steps in Planning a Study

1. **Choose a topic:** Decide what problem you want to study.
2. **State the objective:** Write clearly what you want to find out.
3. **Define the population:** Decide the group you want to study.
4. **Select a sample:** Choose a smaller group from the population.
5. **Collect data:** Use questionnaires, interviews, or records.
6. **Organise data:** Edit, code, and tabulate the information.
7. **Analyse data:** Use tables, graphs, and statistical methods.
8. **Report results:** Write the findings in simple language.

## Some Important Terms

- **Population:** The entire group about which we want information.
- **Sample:** A smaller part of the population that is actually studied.
- **Variable:** Any characteristic that can take different values. For example, height, weight, age, and income are variables.
- **Parameter:** A number that describes the whole population.
- **Statistic:** A number that describes the sample.

*Example:* If you want to know the average height of all students in your university, all students are the population. If you measure only 100 students, those 100 form the sample. The average height of those 100 students is a statistic.

# 3.2 Primary and Secondary Data

> When you collect information for the first time, it is called primary data. When you use information that someone else has already collected, it is called secondary data. Both types are useful. A student who counts the number of bicycles in the college parking lot is collecting primary data. A student who reads last year’s attendance records is using secondary data. Knowing the difference helps you choose the right source for your study.

## Primary Data

**Primary data** is data collected directly by the researcher for the first time for a specific purpose.

### Methods of Collecting Primary Data

**1. Direct observation:** The researcher watches and records events as they happen.

*Example:* Counting the number of customers entering a medical shop in one hour.

**2. Personal interview:** The researcher asks questions face-to-face.

*Example:* A health worker visits homes and asks mothers about child vaccination.

**3. Questionnaire:** A set of written questions is given to respondents. They fill it themselves.

*Example:* A printed form given to students to find out their study habits.

**4. Schedule:** A trained investigator fills in the answers while asking questions.

*Example:* A census enumerator asks questions and writes answers on a schedule form.

**5. Experiment:** The researcher controls conditions to study the effect of one variable on another.

*Example:* Testing three types of seeds to see which gives the highest yield under the same conditions.

### Advantages of Primary Data

- It is collected for a specific purpose.
- It is more reliable because the researcher knows how it was collected.
- It can be collected in the form needed.

### Disadvantages of Primary Data

- It takes more time.
- It costs more money.
- It needs trained people to collect it.

## Secondary Data

**Secondary data** is data that has already been collected by someone else for some other purpose. It is available in published or unpublished form.

### Sources of Secondary Data

- Government reports such as Census of India and NSS reports
- Publications of ministries and departments
- Books, journals, and magazines
- Records of schools, hospitals, and companies
- Websites and online databases
- Reports of banks, universities, and research organisations

### Advantages of Secondary Data

- It saves time and money.
- It is useful for background reading.
- It helps in comparing present data with past data.

### Disadvantages of Secondary Data

- It may not match the exact need of your study.
- The accuracy may not be known.
- It may be out of date.

## Difference Between Primary and Secondary Data

| Feature | Primary Data | Secondary Data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who collects it | The researcher | Someone else |
| Purpose | For the present study | For some other study |
| Cost | Usually high | Usually low |
| Time | Takes more time | Available quickly |
| Reliability | Known to the researcher | May be uncertain |
| Examples | Survey, interview, experiment | Census, NSS, reports |

# 3.3 Survey Questionnaire Construction

> A questionnaire is one of the most common tools used to collect primary data. It is a list of questions given to people to answer. A good questionnaire is short, clear, and easy to answer. A bad questionnaire confuses people and gives wrong answers. In this section, we learn how to make a good questionnaire step by step.

## What is a Questionnaire?

A **questionnaire** is a written set of questions used to collect information from a large number of people. It can be given on paper or online.

*Example:* A college wants to know why students are often late. It prepares a questionnaire with questions about travel time, class timing, and hostel facilities.

## Steps in Constructing a Questionnaire

**Step 1. Define the purpose.** Decide exactly what information you need.

*Example:* If you are studying mobile phone use among students, your purpose is to know how many hours they use phones daily and for what purpose.

**Step 2. Decide the target group.** Choose who will answer the questions.

*Example:* The target group may be all first-year B.Sc. students in your college.

**Step 3. Choose the type of questions.** Decide whether to use open-ended, closed, or scale questions.

**Step 4. Write the questions.** Use simple language. Avoid long or confusing questions.

**Step 5. Arrange the questions.** Start with easy questions. Keep related questions together.

**Step 6. Pre-test the questionnaire.** Try it on a small group. Find out which questions are unclear. Make corrections.

**Step 7. Finalise and use.** Print or share the final version.

![Diagram 2: Steps in Constructing a Questionnaire](images/unit3_diagram2_questionnaire_steps.svg)

*Figure 3.2: Steps in building a good questionnaire.*

## Types of Questions

**Open-ended questions:** The respondent can answer in his or her own words.

*Example:* "What do you like most about your college?"

**Closed-ended questions:** The respondent chooses from given options.

*Example:* "Do you use a bicycle to come to college? Yes / No"

**Multiple-choice questions:** The respondent selects one or more answers from a list.

*Example:* "How do you travel to college? (a) Bus (b) Bicycle (c) Walking (d) Two-wheeler"

**Dichotomous questions:** These have only two answers, usually yes or no.

*Example:* "Do you have a library card? Yes / No"

**Rating scale questions:** The respondent gives a score on a scale.

*Example:* "Rate the college canteen on a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 is poor and 5 is excellent."

## Good Practices in Questionnaire Design

- Keep the questionnaire short.
- Use simple words.
- Ask one thing in one question.
- Avoid leading questions that suggest an answer.
- Begin with easy and interesting questions.
- Put personal or sensitive questions at the end.
- Provide clear instructions.
- Test the questionnaire before final use.

## Mistakes to Avoid

- Do not use difficult words.
- Do not ask two questions in one sentence.
- Do not use vague words like "often" or "many" without explanation.
- Do not ask questions that hurt the respondent’s feelings unless necessary.
- Do not make the questionnaire too long.

*Example of a bad question:* "Don’t you think the college library is very bad and should be improved?"

This question leads the respondent to say yes. A better question is: "How would you rate the college library? Excellent / Good / Average / Poor."

# 3.4 Data Organisation

> After collecting data, the next step is to organise it. Raw data is like a heap of stones. It has value, but it is hard to use. Organising data means cleaning it, classifying it, and putting it in tables. This makes the data easy to understand and analyse. In this section, we learn the basic steps of data organisation.

## Editing

**Editing** means checking the collected data for mistakes and removing errors. It also means completing missing information if possible.

*Example:* If a respondent wrote "150" for age, it is clearly wrong. During editing, you check and correct it or remove it.

## Coding

**Coding** means giving numbers or symbols to answers so that they can be entered into a computer.

*Example:* For the question "Gender," you may code Male as 1 and Female as 2. For "Yes / No" answers, you may code Yes as 1 and No as 0.

## Classification

**Classification** means arranging data into groups or classes based on similarities.

*Example:* If you have heights of 50 students, you can classify them into groups like 150–160 cm, 161–170 cm, and 171–180 cm.

## Tabulation

**Tabulation** means presenting data in the form of tables. A table has rows and columns. It makes comparison easy.

*Example:*

| Marks | Number of Students |
| --- | --- |
| 0–20 | 4 |
| 21–40 | 8 |
| 41–60 | 15 |
| 61–80 | 12 |
| 81–100 | 6 |

## Frequency Distribution

A **frequency distribution** shows how often each value or group of values occurs in the data.

*Example:* If 20 students are asked how many hours they study daily, the frequency distribution may look like this:

| Study Hours | Number of Students |
| --- | --- |
| 0–2 | 5 |
| 3–4 | 10 |
| 5–6 | 4 |
| 7 or more | 1 |

## Steps in Data Organisation

1. Check and edit the data.
2. Code the answers into numbers.
3. Classify the data into groups.
4. Tabulate the data in tables.
5. Prepare frequency distributions.

# 3.5 Data Analysis Workflows in SPSS and R

> Today, most statistical work is done on computers. Two popular software packages are SPSS and R. SPSS is menu-based. You click buttons to do analysis. R is free software where you type commands. Both are useful for entering data, cleaning it, analysing it, and making graphs. In this section, we learn the basic steps of data analysis using these tools.

## What is SPSS?

**SPSS** stands for Statistical Package for the Social Sciences. It is a software used for data entry, data cleaning, analysis, and graph making. It is popular among beginners because it uses menus and buttons.

*Example:* To find the average marks of a class, you can enter the marks in SPSS, click "Analyse," then "Descriptive Statistics," and then "Frequencies" or "Descriptives."

## What is R?

**R** is a free and open-source software used for statistical analysis and graphics. It uses commands or scripts. It is very powerful and is widely used by researchers and data scientists.

*Example:* In R, you can find the average of marks by typing a simple command like `mean(marks)`.

## Basic Data Analysis Workflow

Both SPSS and R follow the same general steps:

1. **Enter or import data:** Type data directly or import from Excel or CSV files.
2. **Clean the data:** Check for errors, missing values, and outliers.
3. **Analyse the data:** Calculate means, percentages, standard deviations, or run tests.
4. **Make graphs:** Draw bar charts, pie charts, histograms, or box plots.
5. **Interpret results:** Explain what the numbers and graphs mean.

![Diagram 3: Basic Data Analysis Workflow in SPSS and R](images/unit3_diagram3_analysis_workflow.svg)

*Figure 3.3: The same workflow is used in both SPSS and R, though the way you give commands differs.*

## Comparing SPSS and R

| Feature | SPSS | R |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Paid software | Free |
| Ease of use | Menu-based, easy for beginners | Command-based, needs practice |
| Best for | Social sciences, health sciences | Research, data science, advanced analysis |
| Graphs | Good | Excellent |
| Repeating analysis | Use saved syntax files | Use saved scripts |

## Simple Example

Suppose we have marks of 5 students: 45, 56, 62, 70, and 78.

**In SPSS:**
- Enter the marks in one column.
- Click Analyse → Descriptive Statistics → Descriptives.
- Select the marks column and click OK.
- SPSS will show the mean, which is 62.2.

**In R:**
- Type: `marks <- c(45, 56, 62, 70, 78)`
- Type: `mean(marks)`
- R will show: `[1] 62.2`

Both give the same answer. The difference is only in how you ask the software.

# 3.6 Overview of National Sample Survey (NSS)

> India is a large country. It is not possible to survey every household every time we need data. The National Sample Survey solves this problem. It selects a small but representative group of households from across the country. Then it collects information from them. This information is used by the government to plan schools, hospitals, jobs, and welfare schemes.

## What is the National Sample Survey?

The **National Sample Survey** is a large-scale survey conducted by the Government of India. It collects information on many topics such as employment, health, education, consumption, and farming. The survey is now carried out by the National Statistical Office.

## Objectives of NSS

- To collect useful statistics about the social and economic condition of people.
- To help the government plan policies and programmes.
- To provide data for research and planning.
- To estimate national and state-level figures from a sample.

*Example:* NSS data tells us how many people in India are employed, how much households spend on food, and how many people use clean drinking water.

## NSS Sampling Method

The NSS uses a method called **stratified multi-stage sampling**. Let us break this into simple parts.

### Stratification

The country is divided into groups called **strata**. Usually, each district is a stratum. Urban and rural areas are often treated separately.

### Multi-Stage Sampling

The sample is selected in stages:

- **First stage:** Villages or urban blocks are selected from each stratum.
- **Second stage:** Households are selected from each selected village or block.
- Sometimes a third stage is used to select persons from households.

This method is practical because India is very large and diverse.

![Diagram 4: NSS Stratified Multi-Stage Sampling](images/unit3_diagram4_nss_sampling.svg)

*Figure 3.4: Simplified view of NSS stratified multi-stage sampling.*

## Examples of NSS Subjects

Different rounds of NSS collect data on different topics. Some common subjects are:

- Consumer expenditure
- Employment and unemployment
- Health and morbidity
- Education
- Land holdings and agriculture
- Housing conditions
- Drinking water and sanitation

## Why is NSS Important?

- It gives reliable data at low cost.
- It covers the whole country.
- It helps compare different states and regions.
- It supports government planning.

# 3.7 Solved Examples

## Example 1: Identifying Primary and Secondary Data

A student writes a project on water usage in her hostel. She measures the amount of water used in the hostel tank for one week. She also reads a government report on water supply in the city. Which is primary data and which is secondary data?

**Solution:**

The water measurements made by the student are **primary data**. The government report is **secondary data** because it was collected by someone else.

## Example 2: Choosing a Data Collection Method

A researcher wants to know why farmers in a village do not use a new variety of seeds. Which method is suitable?

**Solution:**

A **personal interview** is suitable. The researcher can visit the farmers, ask questions, and understand their reasons in detail.

## Example 3: Correcting a Bad Question

The question "Do you not agree that our college has the best library?" is asked in a survey. What is wrong with it?

**Solution:**

This question is leading. It suggests that the library is the best. A better question is: "How do you rate the college library? Excellent / Good / Average / Poor."

## Example 4: Coding Data

In a survey, respondents are asked about their education level. The answers are: illiterate, primary, secondary, graduate. Suggest codes.

**Solution:**

We can code the answers as follows:

| Education Level | Code |
| --- | --- |
| Illiterate | 0 |
| Primary | 1 |
| Secondary | 2 |
| Graduate | 3 |

## Example 5: Classifying Data

The ages of 10 students are: 17, 18, 19, 17, 20, 21, 18, 19, 20, 18. Prepare a frequency distribution.

**Solution:**

| Age | Number of Students |
| --- | --- |
| 17 | 2 |
| 18 | 3 |
| 19 | 2 |
| 20 | 2 |
| 21 | 1 |

## Example 6: NSS Sampling Stage

In an NSS survey, villages are first selected from districts. Then households are selected from the selected villages. What type of sampling is this?

**Solution:**

This is **stratified multi-stage sampling**. Districts are strata, villages are first-stage units, and households are second-stage units.

## Part E: Subjective and Analytical Questions (10 Questions with Detailed Solutions)

### Q1. What is a research design? Why is it important?

**Solution:**

A research design is the plan of a study. It tells us what to study, how to collect data, and how to analyse it. It is important because it keeps the study organised, saves time and money, and helps us get correct and useful results.

### Q2. Distinguish between primary and secondary data. Give two examples of each.

**Solution:**

Primary data is collected directly by the researcher for the present study. Examples: a student survey, measurements made in a science experiment.

Secondary data is already collected by someone else for another purpose. Examples: Census of India report, NSS report, school attendance records.

### Q3. Describe four methods of collecting primary data.

**Solution:**

1. **Observation:** Watching and recording events as they happen.
2. **Interview:** Asking questions face-to-face.
3. **Questionnaire:** Giving written questions to respondents.
4. **Schedule:** A trained investigator asks questions and records answers.

### Q4. What are the steps in constructing a questionnaire?

**Solution:**

1. Define the purpose of the survey.
2. Decide who will answer.
3. Choose the type of questions.
4. Write clear and simple questions.
5. Arrange questions in a logical order.
6. Pre-test the questionnaire on a small group.
7. Make corrections and finalise.

### Q5. What is the difference between open-ended and closed-ended questions? Give one example of each.

**Solution:**

**Open-ended questions** allow the respondent to answer freely. Example: "What do you like about your college?"

**Closed-ended questions** give fixed options. Example: "Do you use the college bus? Yes / No."

### Q6. What are the steps in organising data after collection?

**Solution:**

1. **Editing:** Check for errors and missing values.
2. **Coding:** Convert answers into numbers or symbols.
3. **Classification:** Group similar data together.
4. **Tabulation:** Present data in tables.
5. **Frequency distribution:** Count how often each value occurs.

### Q7. Compare SPSS and R in four points.

**Solution:**

| Point | SPSS | R |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost | Paid | Free |
| Use | Menu-based | Command-based |
| Best for | Beginners and social sciences | Advanced analysis and graphics |
| Repeating work | Syntax files | Scripts |

### Q8. What is the National Sample Survey? Why is it important?

**Solution:**

The National Sample Survey is a large survey conducted by the Government of India. It collects information on employment, health, education, consumption, and other topics. It is important because it gives reliable data at low cost and helps the government plan policies and welfare schemes.

### Q9. Explain stratified multi-stage sampling as used by NSS.

**Solution:**

In stratified multi-stage sampling, the population is first divided into groups called strata, such as districts. Then the sample is selected in stages. In the first stage, villages or urban blocks are selected. In the second stage, households are selected from those villages or blocks. This method is practical for a large country like India.

# 3.9 Summary

Research design is the plan that guides a study. It includes defining the objective, population, sample, and methods of data collection.

Data can be primary or secondary. Primary data is collected first-hand through observation, interview, questionnaire, schedule, or experiment. Secondary data comes from already published sources.

A questionnaire is a written tool for collecting primary data. It should be clear, short, and unbiased. Questions can be open-ended, closed-ended, dichotomous, multiple-choice, or rating scale.

After collection, data must be edited, coded, classified, tabulated, and presented as frequency distributions.

SPSS and R are two popular software packages for data analysis. SPSS is menu-based and easy for beginners. R is free and command-based, and is powerful for analysis and graphs.

The National Sample Survey uses stratified multi-stage sampling to collect data from households across India. It helps the government plan policies and programmes.
