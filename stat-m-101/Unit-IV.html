Time-to-event outcomes, right censoring mechanics, survival & hazard functions, Kaplan-Meier estimator, Greenwood formula, Log-Rank test, Cox Proportional Hazards & Reliability Ageing Classes

4.1 Introduction to Survival Analysis

Suppose a doctor treats ten cancer patients with a new drug. After six months, some patients die, some are still alive, and one patient shifts to another city and cannot be traced. If we only count the patients who died, we ignore those who are still alive. We also cannot say that the missing patient died. We only know that he survived until he left. This kind of data, where we do not know the exact time of the event for everyone, needs special methods. Survival analysis is the branch of statistics that deals with such data. It is used not only for death but for any event that happens over time, such as recovery, relapse, machine failure, or getting a job.
Figure 4.1: Time-to-Event and Censoring Mechanics
Comparison of complete time-to-event failure vs administrative right censoring and drop-out censoring.
What is Survival Analysis?
Survival analysis encompasses statistical methodologies for analyzing time-to-event data where the outcome of interest is the time duration $T$ until a specified event (e.g. death, disease recurrence, mechanical breakdown) occurs, accounting for incomplete follow-up (censoring).

Why Standard Statistical Methods Fail

Ordinary statistical techniques (such as linear regression or standard ANOVA) cannot be directly applied to time-to-event data due to three major characteristics:

  • Censoring: Unobserved exact event times for participants who do not experience the event during follow-up.
  • Skewness: Survival times $T$ are non-negative and typically positive-skewed, violating normality assumptions.
  • Time-Varying Risk: Risk of event occurrence varies continuously over follow-up duration.

Types of Censoring

  • Right Censoring: The true event time is strictly greater than the observed follow-up time ($T > t$). Occurs due to study termination or patient withdrawal.
  • Left Censoring: The event is known to have occurred prior to a specified observation time ($T < t$), but the exact onset date is unknown.
  • Interval Censoring: The event is known to occur within a specific time interval $(t_a, t_b]$, common in periodic screening examinations.

4.1 सर्वाइवल एनालिसिस का परिचय (Introduction to Survival Analysis)

मान लीजिए एक डॉक्टर दस कैंसर रोगियों का इलाज एक नई दवा से करता है। छह महीने बाद, कुछ मरीज मर जाते हैं, कुछ अभी भी जीवित हैं, और एक मरीज दूसरे शहर चला जाता है जिसे ट्रैक नहीं किया जा सकता। यदि हम केवल मरने वाले रोगियों की गिनती करते हैं, तो हम उन लोगों की उपेक्षा करते हैं जो अभी भी जीवित हैं। इस प्रकार के डेटा के लिए जहाँ हर किसी के लिए घटना का सटीक समय नहीं मालूम होता, विशेष तरीकों की आवश्यकता होती है। सर्वाइवल एनालिसिस (survival analysis) सांख्यिकी की वह शाखा है जो ऐसे डेटा से निपटती है।
Survival Analysis की परिभाषा
Survival analysis सांख्यिकीय विधियों का एक समूह है जिसका उपयोग किसी रुचि की घटना (event) के घटित होने तक के समय ($T$) का अध्ययन करने के लिए किया जाता है।

Censoring के प्रकार (Types of Censoring)

  • Right Censoring (दायाँ सेंसरिंग): सबसे आम प्रकार है। यह तब होता है जब अध्ययन के अंत तक घटना नहीं घटित होती है या व्यक्ति फॉलो-अप से बाहर हो जाता है ($T > t$)।
  • Left Censoring (बायाँ सेंसरिंग): तब होता है जब हम जानते हैं कि घटना किसी निश्चित समय से पहले हुई है, लेकिन सटीक समय नहीं मालूम।
  • Interval Censoring (अंतराल सेंसरिंग): तब होता है जब घटना दो समय बिंदुओं के बीच घटित होती है ($(t_a, t_b]$)।

4.2 Basic Survival Functions

Survival data are characterized by three fundamental, mathematically linked functions:

1. Survival Function $S(t)$
The probability that a subject survives beyond time $t$: $$ S(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - F(t) $$ Properties: $S(0) = 1$, non-increasing function, $\lim_{t \to \infty} S(t) = 0$.
2. Hazard Function $h(t)$ (Force of Mortality)
The instantaneous rate of event occurrence at time $t$, given survival up to time $t$: $$ h(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t + \Delta t \mid T \ge t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)} = -\frac{d}{dt}\ln S(t) $$
3. Cumulative Hazard Function $H(t)$
The integrated risk accumulated up to time $t$: $$ H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\,du = -\ln S(t) \implies S(t) = \exp[-H(t)] $$
Figure 4.2: Survival Function S(t) vs Cumulative Hazard H(t)
Inverse relation between monotonically decreasing survival probability S(t) and monotonically increasing cumulative hazard H(t).
Numerical Example 4.2.1: Mathematical Derivation of Survival Functions

Problem: Suppose survival times follow an exponential distribution with constant hazard rate $h(t) = \lambda = 0.05 \text{ year}^{-1}$. Calculate: (a) Survival probability at 5 years $S(5)$, (b) Cumulative probability of death $F(5)$, (c) Probability density $f(5)$, (d) Cumulative hazard $H(5)$, and (e) Median survival time $t_{0.50}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Calculate Cumulative Hazard $H(5)$ and Survival $S(5)$:

$$ H(5) = \int_0^5 0.05 \, du = 0.05 \times 5 = 0.25 $$ $$ S(5) = \exp[-H(5)] = e^{-0.25} = 0.7788 \text{ (77.88\% 5-year survival rate)} $$

Step 2: Calculate CDF $F(5)$ and Density $f(5)$:

$$ F(5) = 1 - S(5) = 1 - 0.7788 = 0.2212 \text{ (22.12\% cumulative mortality)} $$ $$ f(5) = h(5) \times S(5) = 0.05 \times 0.7788 = 0.03894 $$

Step 3: Calculate Median Survival Time $t_{0.50}$:

$$ S(t_{0.50}) = 0.50 \implies e^{-\lambda t_{0.50}} = 0.50 \implies t_{0.50} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.69315}{0.05} = 13.86 \text{ years.} $$

4.2 मूल सर्वाइवल फलन (Basic Survival Functions)

1. Survival Function $S(t)$
संभावना कि कोई व्यक्ति समय $t$ से अधिक समय तक जीवित रहता है: $$ S(t) = P(T > t) = 1 - F(t) $$ शुरुआत में $S(0) = 1$ होता है, और समय बीतने के साथ $S(t)$ घटता है।
2. Hazard Function $h(t)$ (विफलता दर / जोखिम)
समय $t$ पर घटना घटित होने का तात्कालिक जोखिम (instantaneous risk): $$ h(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \le T < t + \Delta t \mid T \ge t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)} = -\frac{d}{dt}\ln S(t) $$
3. Cumulative Hazard Function $H(t)$
समय $t$ तक संचित कुल जोखिम: $$ H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)\,du = -\ln S(t) \implies S(t) = \exp[-H(t)] $$

4.3 Life Table or Actuarial Estimator

The actuarial life table method partitions follow-up into fixed time intervals $[t_{i-1}, t_i)$. For an interval with $n_i$ at risk at start, $d_i$ deaths, and $c_i$ censored cases during interval, Cutler-Ederer adjustment calculates the effective sample size at risk:

$$ n_i' = n_i - \frac{c_i}{2} $$ $$ \hat{p}_i = 1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i'}, \quad \hat{S}(t_i) = \prod_{j=1}^i \hat{p}_j $$
Figure 4.3: Actuarial Life Table Survival Progression
Step-by-step multiplication of conditional survival rates across consecutive life table intervals.
Numerical Example 4.3.1: Full Actuarial Life Table Construction

Problem: Calculate the cumulative actuarial survival probabilities $\hat{S}(t)$ for a 100-patient cohort over 3 yearly intervals given: Interval 0–1y ($n_1=100, d_1=10, c_1=6$), Interval 1–2y ($n_2=84, d_2=8, c_2=4$), Interval 2–3y ($n_3=72, d_3=6, c_3=4$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Interval 0–1 Year:

$$ n_1' = 100 - \frac{6}{2} = 97.0 \implies \hat{p}_1 = 1 - \frac{10}{97.0} = 0.8969 \implies \hat{S}(1) = 0.8969 $$

Step 2: Interval 1–2 Years:

$$ n_2' = 84 - \frac{4}{2} = 82.0 \implies \hat{p}_2 = 1 - \frac{8}{82.0} = 0.9024 \implies \hat{S}(2) = 0.8969 \times 0.9024 = 0.8094 $$

Step 3: Interval 2–3 Years:

$$ n_3' = 72 - \frac{4}{2} = 70.0 \implies \hat{p}_3 = 1 - \frac{6}{70.0} = 0.9143 \implies \hat{S}(3) = 0.8094 \times 0.9143 = 0.7400 $$

Conclusion: Estimated 3-year actuarial survival probability is $\hat{S}(3) = 74.00\%$.

4.3 लाइफ टेबल या एक्ट्यूएरियल विधि (Actuarial Method)

यह विधि समय को निश्चित अंतरालों में विभाजित करती है और प्रत्येक अंतराल के लिए जीवित रहने की संभावना की गणना करती है।

$$ n_i' = n_i - \frac{c_i}{2}, \quad \hat{p}_i = 1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i'}, \quad \hat{S}(t_i) = \prod_{j=1}^i \hat{p}_j $$

4.4 Kaplan-Meier Non-Parametric Estimator

The Kaplan-Meier (Product-Limit) estimator estimates $S(t)$ at exact event times $t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_k$ without requiring arbitrary time interval groupings:

$$ \hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left( 1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i} \right) $$
Greenwood's Variance Formula for Ŝ(t)
$$ \widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(t)] = [\hat{S}(t)]^2 \sum_{t_i \le t} \frac{d_i}{n_i (n_i - d_i)} $$ $$ \text{SE}[\hat{S}(t)] = \sqrt{\widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(t)]} $$

4.4 कपलान-मायर अनुमानक (Kaplan-Meier Estimator)

Kaplan-Meier (Product-Limit) estimator घटनाओं के सटीक समय का उपयोग करके $S(t)$ की गणना करता है।

$$ \hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} \left( 1 - \frac{d_i}{n_i} \right) $$

Greenwood का सूत्र मानक त्रुटि $\text{SE}[\hat{S}(t)]$ प्रदान करता है:

$$ \widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(t)] = [\hat{S}(t)]^2 \sum_{t_i \le t} \frac{d_i}{n_i (n_i - d_i)} $$

4.5 Total Time on Test (TTT)

The Total Time on Test ($TTT$) represents the cumulative operational exposure time aggregated across all $n$ subjects up to failure or censoring:

$$ TTT = \sum_{i=1}^n t_i $$

Under an exponential distribution with constant hazard $\lambda$, the Maximum Likelihood Estimator is $\hat{\lambda} = \frac{D}{TTT}$ where $D$ is total observed failures.

Figure 4.5: Total Time on Test (TTT) Transform Plots

Numerical Example 4.5.1: Total Time on Test & Constant Hazard Estimation

Problem: Eight industrial pumps undergo endurance testing. Observed operational times in hours: 120, 180, 240, 310+, 400, 450+, 520, 600+ (where + denotes right censoring at study termination). Calculate: (a) Total Time on Test ($TTT$), (b) MLE of constant failure rate $\hat{\lambda}$, and (c) Mean Time To Failure ($\text{MTTF}$).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Calculate Total Time on Test ($TTT$):

$$ TTT = 120 + 180 + 240 + 310 + 400 + 450 + 520 + 600 = 2,820 \text{ pump-hours.} $$

Step 2: Calculate Number of Failures ($D$) and MLE Hazard ($\hat{\lambda}$):

$$ D = 5 \text{ failures (Uncensored cases: 120, 180, 240, 400, 520)} $$ $$ \hat{\lambda} = \frac{D}{TTT} = \frac{5}{2,820} = 0.001773 \text{ failures per hour.} $$

Step 3: Calculate Mean Time To Failure ($\text{MTTF}$):

$$ \text{MTTF} = \frac{1}{\hat{\lambda}} = \frac{1}{0.001773} = 564.0 \text{ operational hours.} $$

4.5 कुल परीक्षण समय (Total Time on Test - TTT)

TTT सभी व्यक्तियों द्वारा अध्ययन में बिताए गए कुल समय का योग है।

$$ TTT = \sum_{i=1}^n t_i $$

TTT प्लॉट डेटा के निरंतर जोखिम (constant hazard / exponential distribution) की जांच करने में मदद करता है।

4.6 Log-Rank Test and Cox Proportional Hazards Regression

1. Two-Sample Log-Rank Test

Tests $H_0: S_1(t) = S_2(t)$ by comparing observed event counts $O_1 = \sum d_{1j}$ against expected event counts $E_1 = \sum n_{1j} \frac{d_j}{n_j}$ under $H_0$:

$$ \chi^2_{\text{log-rank}} = \frac{(O_1 - E_1)^2}{\text{Var}(O_1 - E_1)} \sim \chi^2_1 $$

2. Cox Proportional Hazards Model

Semi-parametric model expressing hazard as a function of baseline hazard $h_0(t)$ and covariates $X$:

$$ h(t \mid X) = h_0(t) \exp(\beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \beta_p X_p) $$ $$ \text{Hazard Ratio (HR)} = \frac{h(t \mid X=1)}{h(t \mid X=0)} = \exp(\beta) $$

Numerical Example 4.6.2: Cox Model Hazard Ratio & Confidence Interval

Problem: In a clinical trial of lung cancer, the treatment variable $X$ (1 = Immunotherapy, 0 = Standard Chemotherapy) yields $\hat{\beta} = -0.5108$ with $\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}) = 0.2200$. Calculate the Hazard Ratio (HR) and its $95\%$ Confidence Interval.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \text{HR} = \exp(\hat{\beta}) = \exp(-0.5108) = 0.6000 \text{ (40\% reduction in risk of death)} $$ $$ \text{Lower 95\% CI} = \exp(-0.5108 - 1.96 \times 0.2200) = \exp(-0.9420) = 0.3898 $$ $$ \text{Upper 95\% CI} = \exp(-0.5108 + 1.96 \times 0.2200) = \exp(-0.0796) = 0.9235 $$

Conclusion: $\text{HR} = 0.60$ ($95\%$ CI: 0.390 to 0.924). Since the CI does not include 1.0, Immunotherapy significantly reduces mortality risk.

4.6 लॉग-रैंक परीक्षण और कॉक्स आनुपातिक जोखिम मॉडल

Log-Rank Test: दो या दो से अधिक समूहों के सर्वाइवल कर्व्स की तुलना करता है ($H_0: S_1(t) = S_2(t)$)।

Cox Proportional Hazards Model: सेमी-पैरामीट्रिक मॉडल जो कोवेरिएट्स के प्रभाव को Hazard Ratio (HR = $e^{\beta}$) के रूप में मापता है:

$$ h(t \mid X) = h_0(t) \exp(\beta_1 X_1 + \dots + \beta_p X_p) $$

4.7 Ageing Classes of Life Distributions

Reliability analysis categorizes lifetime distributions based on hazard evolution over time:

  • IFR (Increasing Failure Rate): $h(t)$ increases monotonically with time.
  • IFRA (Increasing Failure Rate Average): $\frac{1}{t}\int_0^t h(u)du$ increases with time.
  • NBU (New Better than Used): $S(x+t) \le S(x) S(t)$ for all $x, t \ge 0$.
  • NBUE (New Better than Used in Expectation): $e(t) \le e(0)$.
Figure 4.7: Implication Hierarchy of Positive Ageing Classes
Strict nested hierarchy of positive reliability ageing classes: IFR ⊆ IFRA ⊆ NBU ⊆ NBUE.
Hierarchy of Positive Ageing Classes
$$ IFR \implies IFRA \implies NBU \implies NBUE $$ Dual Negative Ageing Classes: $DFR \implies DFRA \implies NWU \implies NWUE$.
Numerical Example 4.7.1: Verification of Weibull Ageing Class

Problem: A machine component has a Weibull lifetime distribution with hazard rate $h(t) = \alpha \lambda t^{\alpha - 1}$ where $\lambda = 0.02$ and shape parameter $\alpha = 1.5$. Determine whether the component belongs to the IFR ageing class.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Evaluate the derivative of the hazard function $h'(t)$:

$$ h(t) = 1.5 \times 0.02 \times t^{1.5 - 1} = 0.03 \, t^{0.5} $$ $$ h'(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(0.03 \, t^{0.5}) = 0.03 \times 0.5 \, t^{-0.5} = \frac{0.015}{\sqrt{t}} $$

Step 2: Check sign of $h'(t)$ for all $t > 0$:

$$ \text{For all } t > 0, \quad h'(t) = \frac{0.015}{\sqrt{t}} > 0 $$

Conclusion: Since $h'(t) > 0$ strictly for all $t > 0$, the hazard function $h(t)$ is monotonically increasing with time. Therefore, the Weibull distribution with $\alpha = 1.5 > 1$ belongs to the **IFR (Increasing Failure Rate)** class.

4.7 एजिंग वर्ग (Ageing Classes)

उम्र बढ़ने के साथ विफलता जोखिम में परिवर्तन के आधार पर वर्गीकरण: $IFR \implies IFRA \implies NBU \implies NBUE$।

$$ \sum \frac{d_i}{n_i(n_i-d_i)} = 0.01786 + 0.05714 + 0.08333 = 0.15833 $$ $$ \widehat{\text{Var}}[\hat{S}(8)] = (0.4688)^2 \times 0.15833 = 0.21977 \times 0.15833 = 0.03479 $$ $$ \text{SE}[\hat{S}(8)] = \sqrt{0.03479} = 0.1865 \implies \hat{S}(8) = 0.4688 \pm 0.3656 \text{ (95\% CI: 0.103 to 0.834)} $$
Example 4.8.2: Cox Model Hazard Ratio & Confidence Interval

Problem: In a clinical trial of lung cancer, the treatment variable $X$ (1 = Immunotherapy, 0 = Standard Chemotherapy) yields $\hat{eta} = -0.5108$ with $\text{SE}(\hat{eta}) = 0.2200$. Calculate the Hazard Ratio (HR) and its $95\%$ Confidence Interval.

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \text{HR} = \exp(\hat{\beta}) = \exp(-0.5108) = 0.6000 \text{ (40\% reduction in risk of death)} $$ $$ \text{Lower 95\% CI} = \exp(-0.5108 - 1.96 \times 0.2200) = \exp(-0.9420) = 0.3898 $$ $$ \text{Upper 95\% CI} = \exp(-0.5108 + 1.96 \times 0.2200) = \exp(-0.0796) = 0.9235 $$

Conclusion: $\text{HR} = 0.60$ ($95\%$ CI: 0.390 to 0.924). Since the CI does not include 1.0, Immunotherapy significantly reduces mortality risk.

Part E: Subjective and Analytical Questions (10 Questions with Detailed Solutions)

Q1. What is survival analysis? Give two examples where it can be used.

Solution:

Survival analysis is a collection of statistical procedures for analyzing time-to-event data where follow-up times may be right-censored. Examples: 1. Estimating patient survival time after organ transplantation. 2. Modeling industrial battery lifetime until failure.

Q2. Explain the three types of censoring with examples.

Solution:

Right Censoring: Event time $T > t_{\text{end}}$ (e.g. patient alive at study completion).
Left Censoring: Event time $T < t_0$ (e.g. disease infection occurred prior to blood screening).
Interval Censoring: Event time $T \in (t_a, t_b]$ (e.g. tumor recurrence detected between 6-month checkups).

Q3. Define survival function, hazard function, and cumulative hazard function. How are they related?

Solution:

$S(t) = P(T > t)$, $h(t) = f(t)/S(t)$, $H(t) = \int_0^t h(u)du$. Interrelationships: $S(t) = \exp[-H(t)]$, $h(t) = - rac{d}{dt}\ln S(t)$, $F(t) = 1 - S(t)$.

Q4. Describe the actuarial method of estimating survival probability.

Solution:

Divides follow-up into fixed intervals $[t_{i-1}, t_i)$. Adjusts risk set $n_i' = n_i - c_i/2$. Calculates interval survival $\hat{p}_i = 1 - d_i/n_i'$ and cumulative survival $\hat{S}(t_i) = \prod \hat{p}_j$.

Q5. Describe the Kaplan-Meier estimator.

Solution:

The Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimator uses exact event times $t_i$: $\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{t_i \le t} (1 - d_i/n_i)$. It forms a step function with variance given by Greenwood's formula.

Q6. What is total time on test? How is it used?

Solution:

$TTT = \sum_{i=1}^n t_i$. Used in TTT plots to evaluate constant hazard (linear), increasing hazard (concave/IFR), or decreasing hazard (convex/DFR).

Q7. What is the log-rank test? When is it used?

Solution:

Non-parametric test comparing observed vs expected events across treatment groups ($H_0: S_1(t) = S_2(t)$). Used under proportional hazards assumptions.

Q8. Define IFR, IFRA, NBU, and NBUE.

Solution:

IFR: Increasing $h(t)$. IFRA: Increasing average hazard. NBU: $S(x+t) \le S(x)S(t)$. NBUE: $e(t) \le e(0)$.

Q9. State the hierarchy among IFR, IFRA, NBU, and NBUE. What are the dual classes?

Solution:

Hierarchy: $IFR \implies IFRA \implies NBU \implies NBUE$. Dual classes: $DFR \implies DFRA \implies NWU \implies NWUE$.

Q10. The survival times of five patients are: 3, 5, 5, 7+, 9. Calculate the Kaplan-Meier survival estimates.

Solution:

At $t=3$: $\hat{S}(3) = 1 - 1/5 = 0.8000$.
At $t=5$: $\hat{S}(5) = 0.8000 \times (1 - 2/4) = 0.4000$.
At $t=7+$: Censored ($n_i = 1 \to 0$).
At $t=9$: $\hat{S}(9) = 0.4000 \times (1 - 1/1) = 0.0000$.

4.10 Summary & References

Survival analysis provides specialized non-parametric, semi-parametric, and parametric methodologies for analyzing time-to-event data subject to right censoring. Key tools include the Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimator, Greenwood's variance formula, Actuarial life tables, Log-Rank hypothesis test, and Cox Proportional Hazards regression modeling.
