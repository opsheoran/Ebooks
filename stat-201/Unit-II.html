2.1 Concept of Curve Fitting

All the methods that you have learnt in Block 1 of this course were based on univariate distributions, i.e. all measures analysed only a single variable. But many times we have data for two or more variables and our interest is to know the best functional relationship between variables that given data describes. In this unit, you will learn how to fit various functions such as straight line, parabola of the second degree, power curve, and exponential curves.

Curve fitting has theoretical importance in regression and correlation analysis while practically it is used to present the relationship by a simple algebraic expression. All these methods can be used to estimate the values of the dependent variable for specific values of the independent variable.

In statistics and experimental sciences, we often collect a set of \(n\) paired observations, denoted as \((x_i, y_i)\) for \(i = 1, 2, \dots, n\). When we plot these points on a coordinate plane, they form a scatter diagram. Curve fitting is the mathematical process of finding a specific equation—a "curve" (which could be a straight line or a curved shape)—that best describes the general trend of this scattered data.

Why Do We Fit Curves? (Objectives of Curve Fitting)

  1. Interpolation & Extrapolation: To estimate the value of \(Y\) for a given \(X\) that lies within (interpolation) or outside (extrapolation) the observed data range.
  2. Data Summarisation: To replace a large, complex table of data with a single, compact mathematical formula.
  3. Trend Analysis: To understand the underlying physical, biological, or economic law governing the relationship between the variables.

2.2 Principle of Least Squares

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be the independent and dependent variables respectively, and we have a set of values \((x_i, y_i)\) for \(i = 1, 2, \dots, n\), i.e. observations are taken from \(n\) individuals on \(X\) and \(Y\). We are interested in studying the function \(y = f(x)\).

If \(\hat{y}_i\) is the estimated value of \(y_i\) obtained by the function and \(y_i\) is the observed value of \(Y\) at \(x_i\), then the difference between observed value and estimated value \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\) is called error of the estimate or residual for \(x_i\).

The Principle of Least Squares

The Principle of Least Squares consists in minimizing the sum of squares of the residuals, i.e., according to the principle of least squares:

\[ S = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - f(x_i))^2 = \text{Minimum} \]

Why Do We Square the Errors?

  1. Sign Cancellation: If we simply added the raw errors \(\sum e_i\), positive errors (points above the curve) and negative errors (points below the curve) would cancel each other out. A terrible curve could technically have a sum of errors equal to zero.
  2. Absolute Values are Difficult: We could use the sum of absolute errors \(\sum |e_i|\), but the absolute value function has sharp corners and is not differentiable everywhere, making calculus-based optimization very difficult.
  3. Penalty for Large Errors: Squaring the errors heavily penalizes large outliers, forcing the fitted curve to represent the entirety of the data points fairly.

General Calculus Procedure for Least Squares

Consider a curve of the type \(y = f(x; a_0, a_1, \dots, a_m)\) where \(Y\) is dependent variable, \(X\) is independent variable, and \(a_0, a_1, \dots, a_m\) are unknown constants.

With the given values \((x_i, y_i)\), we determine the constants such that \(S = \sum [y_i - f(x_i; a_0, a_1, \dots, a_m)]^2\) is minimized.

To find the minimum of the sum of squared errors \(S\), we use partial differentiation:

  1. Write \(S\) in terms of the curve's unknown constants (parameters).
  2. Take the partial derivative of \(S\) with respect to each unknown parameter: \(\frac{\partial S}{\partial a_0} = 0, \frac{\partial S}{\partial a_1} = 0, \dots, \frac{\partial S}{\partial a_m} = 0\).
  3. Set each partial derivative to exactly zero (the mathematical condition for a minimum).
  4. Solve the resulting system of simultaneous linear equations universally known as the Normal Equations.

2.3 Fitting a Straight Line (\(Y = a + bX\))

A straight line is the simplest curve, used when the variables exhibit a linear rate of change: \(Y = a + bX\). Here \(a\) is the Y-intercept (value of \(Y\) when \(X = 0\)) and \(b\) is the Slope (rate of change of \(Y\) with respect to \(X\)).

Step-by-Step Derivation of Normal Equations

Let the equation of the best-fit line be \(Y = a + bX\). The sum of squared errors is:

\[ S = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - a - b x_i)^2 \]

Step 1: Differentiate \(S\) with respect to \(a\):

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial a} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - a - b x_i) = 0 \]

Divide by \(-2\) and distribute summation: \(\sum y_i - \sum a - b \sum x_i = 0\). Since \(\sum_{i=1}^n a = n a\):

\[ \mathbf{\sum Y = n a + b \sum X} \quad \text{--- (Normal Equation I)} \]

Step 2: Differentiate \(S\) with respect to \(b\):

\[ \frac{\partial S}{\partial b} = -2 \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i (y_i - a - b x_i) = 0 \]

Divide by \(-2\) and distribute summation: \(\sum x_i y_i - a \sum x_i - b \sum x_i^2 = 0\):

\[ \mathbf{\sum XY = a \sum X + b \sum X^2} \quad \text{--- (Normal Equation II)} \]
Explicit Solution Formulas for \(a\) and \(b\):
\[ b = \frac{n \sum XY - \sum X \sum Y}{n \sum X^2 - (\sum X)^2}, \quad a = \bar{Y} - b \bar{X} \]

where \(\bar{X} = \frac{\sum X}{n}\) and \(\bar{Y} = \frac{\sum Y}{n}\) are arithmetic means.

2.4 Fitting a Second-Degree Parabola (\(Y = a + bX + cX^2\))

When a scatter plot reveals a curve with a single bend (convex or concave), a straight line is insufficient. We fit a second-degree polynomial or parabola: \(Y = a + bX + cX^2\). This model has three unknown parameters: \(a, b, c\).

Derivation of 3 Normal Equations

The sum of squared errors is \(S = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (y_i - a - b x_i - c x_i^2)^2\). Taking partial derivatives:

  1. \(\frac{\partial S}{\partial a} = 0 \implies \mathbf{\sum Y = n a + b \sum X + c \sum X^2}\)
  2. \(\frac{\partial S}{\partial b} = 0 \implies \mathbf{\sum XY = a \sum X + b \sum X^2 + c \sum X^3}\)
  3. \(\frac{\partial S}{\partial c} = 0 \implies \mathbf{\sum X^2 Y = a \sum X^2 + b \sum X^3 + c \sum X^4}\)

The "Change of Origin" Shortcut (\(\sum X = 0\))

If \(X\) values are equally spaced, shift origin to the middle value of \(X\) such that \(\sum X = 0\) and \(\sum X^3 = 0\). The normal equations simplify directly to:

\[ a = \frac{\sum Y - c \sum X^2}{n}, \quad b = \frac{\sum XY}{\sum X^2}, \quad c = \frac{n \sum X^2 Y - \sum Y \sum X^2}{n \sum X^4 - (\sum X^2)^2} \]

2.5 Fitting a Power Curve (\(Y = a X^b\))

The power curve models relationships where one variable scales as a power of another (e.g. \(Y = a X^b\)). To linearize, take common logarithm \(\log_{10}\) on both sides:

\[ \log_{10} Y = \log_{10} a + b \log_{10} X \]
Linearized Form & Normal Equations:

Let \(v = \log_{10} Y\), \(u = \log_{10} X\), \(A = \log_{10} a\), and \(B = b\). The equation becomes \(v = A + B u\).

\[ \begin{cases} \sum v = n A + B \sum u \\ \sum u v = A \sum u + B \sum u^2 \end{cases} \]

Parameter Recovery: \(a = \text{antilog}_{10}(A) = 10^A\), and \(b = B\).

2.6 Fitting Exponential Curves (\(Y = a b^X\) and \(Y = a e^{bX}\))

Form 1: Base-10 Exponential Curve (\(Y = a b^X\))

Take \(\log_{10}\) on both sides: \(\log_{10} Y = \log_{10} a + X \log_{10} b \implies v = A + B X\).

Normal equations: \(\sum v = n A + B \sum X\) and \(\sum X v = A \sum X + B \sum X^2\).

Parameter recovery: \(a = 10^A\), \(b = 10^B\).

Form 2: Natural Exponential Curve (\(Y = a e^{bX}\))

Take natural logarithm \(\ln\) on both sides: \(\ln Y = \ln a + b X \implies v = A + b X\).

Normal equations: \(\sum v = n A + b \sum X\) and \(\sum X v = A \sum X + b \sum X^2\).

Parameter recovery: \(a = e^A\), \(b = b\).

Fundamental Rules for Students

  1. Understand the Calculus: The Normal Equations are not arbitrary; they are the direct mathematical result of setting partial derivatives of the squared-error sum to zero.
  2. Count Your Unknowns: The number of unknown parameters in your curve dictates the number of Normal Equations you must generate and solve.
  3. Linearise First: You can only directly apply Normal Equations to linear formulas. If you have exponents or powers as parameters, linearise using logarithms first.
  4. Tables are Mandatory: Never try to solve these by calculating sums randomly. Always build a strict computation table with columns for \(X, Y, X^2, XY, U, V, \log X, \log Y\), etc., sum the columns at the bottom, and substitute totals into Normal Equations.

📝 Subjective Questions — 30 Questions

Q1. Define Curve Fitting. Discuss its main objectives and practical applications.
Solution:

Curve fitting is the mathematical procedure of finding a specific functional relationship y = f(x) that best describes the trend in a set of n paired observations (x_i, y_i). Objectives: 1) Data Summarisation: Replacing large tabular data with a compact formula. 2) Interpolation & Extrapolation: Estimating Y for X values inside or outside the observed range. 3) Trend Analysis: Identifying underlying physical, biological, or economic laws. Practical applications: Agricultural yield forecasting, crop response to fertilizer, population growth models, and sales forecasting.

Q2. State and explain the Principle of Least Squares. Why do we minimize squared errors instead of simple errors or absolute errors?
Solution:

Principle of Least Squares states that the curve of best fit is the one that minimizes the sum of squared residuals S = Σ (y_i - f(x_i))². Reasons for squaring: 1) Prevents sign cancellation: Simple sum Σ e_i can be zero for terrible fits due to positive and negative errors canceling out. 2) Differentiability: Absolute error function Σ |e_i| has sharp corners at zero and is non-differentiable, making calculus optimization difficult. 3) Outlier penalty: Squaring heavily penalizes large errors, forcing the curve to represent all data points fairly.

Q3. Derive the normal equations for fitting a straight line Y = a + bX using calculus.
Solution:

Let S = Σ e_i² = Σ (y_i - a - b x_i)². To minimize S, take partial derivatives with respect to a and b and set to zero: 1) ∂S/∂a = -2 Σ (y_i - a - b x_i) = 0 ⇒ Σ y_i = n a + b Σ x_i. 2) ∂S/∂b = -2 Σ x_i (y_i - a - b x_i) = 0 ⇒ Σ x_i y_i = a Σ x_i + b Σ x_i². These two equations are the Normal Equations for a straight line.

Q4. Explain the method of change of origin for fitting a straight line when X values are equally spaced.
Solution:

When X values are equally spaced, shift origin to the mid-value A: U = (X - A) / h. This makes Σ U = 0. The normal equations reduce to: 1) Σ Y = n a' ⇒ a' = Σ Y / n. 2) Σ U Y = b' Σ U² ⇒ b' = Σ U Y / Σ U². The line in transformed units is Y = a' + b' U. Converting back: a = a' - b' A / h, b = b' / h.

Q5. Derive the three normal equations for fitting a second-degree parabola Y = a + bX + cX².

Q1. Define Curve Fitting. Discuss its main objectives and practical applications.
Solution:

Curve fitting is the mathematical procedure of finding a specific functional relationship y = f(x) that best describes the trend in a set of n paired observations (x_i, y_i). Objectives: 1) Data Summarisation: Replacing large tabular data with a compact formula. 2) Interpolation & Extrapolation: Estimating Y for X values inside or outside the observed range. 3) Trend Analysis: Identifying underlying physical, biological, or economic laws. Practical applications: Agricultural yield forecasting, crop response to fertilizer, population growth models, and sales forecasting.

Q2. State and explain the Principle of Least Squares. Why do we minimize squared errors instead of simple errors or absolute errors?
Solution:

Principle of Least Squares states that the curve of best fit is the one that minimizes the sum of squared residuals S = Σ (y_i - f(x_i))². Reasons for squaring: 1) Prevents sign cancellation: Simple sum Σ e_i can be zero for terrible fits due to positive and negative errors canceling out. 2) Differentiability: Absolute error function Σ |e_i| has sharp corners at zero and is non-differentiable, making calculus optimization difficult. 3) Outlier penalty: Squaring heavily penalizes large errors, forcing the curve to represent all data points fairly.

Q3. Derive the normal equations for fitting a straight line Y = a + bX using calculus.
Solution:

Let S = Σ e_i² = Σ (y_i - a - b x_i)². To minimize S, take partial derivatives with respect to a and b and set to zero: 1) ∂S/∂a = -2 Σ (y_i - a - b x_i) = 0 ⇒ Σ y_i = n a + b Σ x_i. 2) ∂S/∂b = -2 Σ x_i (y_i - a - b x_i) = 0 ⇒ Σ x_i y_i = a Σ x_i + b Σ x_i². These two equations are the Normal Equations for a straight line.

Q4. Explain the method of change of origin for fitting a straight line when X values are equally spaced.
Solution:

When X values are equally spaced, shift origin to the mid-value A: U = (X - A) / h. This makes Σ U = 0. The normal equations reduce to: 1) Σ Y = n a' ⇒ a' = Σ Y / n. 2) Σ U Y = b' Σ U² ⇒ b' = Σ U Y / Σ U². The line in transformed units is Y = a' + b' U. Converting back: a = a' - b' A / h, b = b' / h.
